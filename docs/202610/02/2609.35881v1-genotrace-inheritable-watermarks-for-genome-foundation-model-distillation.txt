# ABSTRACT 

Can a genome model retain a detectable record of the synthetic sequences used to train it? We study watermark inheritance through distillation with GenoTrace, a codon-aware extension of green-list watermarking. Two token-level factors modulate the teacher’s generation bias using codon position and organism-specific codon usage. The resulting sequences train a smaller student, whose outputs are audited without an active watermark processor. In a three-seed GenomeOcean-500M-to-100M experiment, the joint configuration achieves a mean audit score of 17.88 and 94.5% detection at a fixed threshold. It retains 49.0% detection after key-aware token substitution, compared with 0% for the available single-seed plain-watermark comparator, and 47.0% after combined mechanism-targeted nucleotide edits. Additional experiments establish inherited signal across five organism-conditioned datasets and teacher–student size ratios up to 40. Component ablations and computational sequence-quality assays reveal distinct operating points for detection strength and coding coverage. GenoTrace provides a practical token-level construction and an empirical account of how genomic structure shapes inherited watermark signals. The findings concern shared-tokenizer distillation and the tested editing procedures, with calibration and biological utility treated as separate evaluation requirements. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Genome foundation models make synthetic DNA a reusable resource for downstream learning. Mod-els such as GenomeOcean and Evo span a wide range of parameter scales and genomic contexts (Wu et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2024; Brixi et al., 2026). Their outputs can also become training examples for smaller models. Distillation transfers behavior from a teacher to a student (Hinton et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021; Sanh et al., 2019), but the resulting model does not ordinarily reveal which synthetic corpus influenced its parameters. Detecting that influence would provide a useful component of model provenance. Watermark inheritance supplies a possible trace. A teacher biases its output distribution using a secret key; a student trained on those outputs may learn a residual of the bias. Sander et al. (2024) establish this phenomenon in text models. Genomic data introduce a structural complication: models can share the four-letter DNA alphabet while using different token vocabularies, and subword tokens need not align with codons. A watermark that is easy to learn in token space may also change coding structure or sequence diversity. The design question is therefore how to allocate a watermark bias across genomic tokens, not simply how to make the bias stronger. Consider a provider that supplies synthetic genomic sequences for downstream training. A second party trains a smaller generator on that corpus, then exposes only the student’s outputs. The provider would like to test whether the marked distribution influenced the student without inspecting its parameters. This setting differs from detecting whether one sequence was directly generated by the teacher: the evidence must survive a learning step. It also differs from identifying a copied checkpoint, since the student can have a different architecture or parameter count. GenoTrace addresses this setting by allocating generation bias using genomic structure. Its position factor measures the fraction of a token overlapping third codon positions. Its usage factor measures the fraction of complete codons inside the token belonging to an organism-specific preferred set. 1arXiv:2609.35881v1 [cs.CR] 27 Sep 2026 Preprint.

We organize the study around three questions. Does the signal survive shared-tokenizer distillation, and how does inheritance vary with organism and model scale? How much signal remains after explicitly specified output edits? Which detection operating points preserve more sequence-level quality? These questions require different controls: an unwatermarked student tests background positives, an edited plain-watermark student tests a reference construction, and biological assays evaluate properties that the detector does not measure. Our contributions are the codon-aware token-bias construction, a component-and-strength study with seed-level reporting, and a joint analysis of inherited signal and computational sequence quality. In the main GenomeOcean-500M-to-100M campaign, joint fractional weighting reaches mean audit score 17.88 and 94.5% detection. It retains 49.0% after key-aware token substitution and 47.0% after combined mechanism-targeted nucleotide edits. The extensions and quality analyses explain where these findings generalize and where additional calibration or validation is needed. We treat these as measured operating points, not guarantees of universal attribution or preserved biological function. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Watermark design and inheritance. Green-list watermarking promotes a keyed subset of to-kens during generation (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023). Reliability studies examine editing and par-2Preprint. tial retention (Kirchenbauer et al., 2024), while unbiased and distortion-free constructions target sampling-distribution preservation (Hu et al., 2024; Kuditipudi et al., 2024). Cryptographic unde-tectability (Christ et al., 2024), provable edit robustness (Zhao et al., 2024), semantic partitions (Hou et al., 2024), and deployed sampling watermarks (Dathathri et al., 2024) address different design objectives. Inheritance concerns a further stage: a model trained on watermarked outputs. Sander et al. (2024) show that detection at this stage requires attention to repeated contexts and the statistical unit. We distinguish the observed inherited score from a calibrated provenance decision. 

Attacks and evaluation. Paraphrasing, watermark stealing, and contrastive distillation expose distinct attack surfaces (Krishna et al., 2023; Jovanovi´ c et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2025). Pan et al. (2025) study removal before distillation and neutralization at inference; Diaa et al. (2025) optimize attacks against watermark objectives. SEEK addresses scrubbing and spoofing through vocabulary-side design (Shen et al., 2025). These studies motivate explicit access and quality budgets. Our tests apply specified transformations to student outputs rather than reproducing the complete text-attack training pipelines. 

Genomic and biological provenance. DNAMark and CentralMark learn watermark logits from DNA or protein embeddings (Zhang et al., 2025). DNAMark constrains synonymous choices, whereas CentralMark modifies the second codon base to support detection after translation. FoldMark operates on protein structures (Zhang et al., 2024). GenoTrace instead asks whether generation bias is learned through model training. Genome models also differ substantially in tokenization and architecture (Nguyen et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Dalla-Torre et al., 2025; Sanabria et al., 2024). Codon degeneracy and usage variation motivate our factors (Crick, 1966; Plotkin & Kudla, 2011; Hershberg & Petrov, 2008), but neither factor guarantees functional neutrality. Appendix F situates the broader detection, biological-design, and statistical literature. 

# 3 CODON -AWARE WATERMARK INHERITANCE 

3.1 SETTING AND ACCESS MODEL 

The provider controls teacher sampling and the key used to mark its training outputs. The student trainer receives sequences rather than teacher logits and optimizes next-token prediction. The auditor observes student responses and knows the marking key and teacher tokenizer; student weights and inference logits are unnecessary for the score. Our principal experiments use compatible teacher and student vocabularies so that the same transition-level event can be reconstructed. Neither student evaluation nor auditing injects a new watermark. An output editor sits between student generation and auditing. We separate key-aware token editing from keyless transformations targeted at codon position or usage. These capabilities are not nested: knowing the mechanism is not the same as knowing the key, and inserting bases is not a budget-matched substitute for token replacement. Accordingly, we report each transformation with its access, sampling rate, and source control. Training-time removal, adaptive detector-query optimization, and spoofing by a key holder are different experiments, not implied successes of this evaluation. 3.2 GENERATION AND AUDIT

# 4 EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN 

Training chain. The default teacher is GenomeOcean-500M and the student is GenomeOcean-100M. For each defense and training seed, we generate 2,000 teacher examples, replace 25% with real-genome fragments, fit a student adapter, merge it into the student, and sample evaluation responses without a watermark processor. Replacement keeps the training-set size fixed; it is not an extra 25% of examples. The main real-genome source is E. coli K-12 MG1655. The objective learns from sequences rather than the teacher’s probability vectors, so inheritance must arise through the sampled training corpus. Student optimization uses batch size 4, 4,000 steps, maximum training length 512, LoRA rank 8 and scaling 16, learning rate 2 × 10 −4, and weight decay 0.01. A 200-step warm-up precedes cosine decay to a 0.05 learning-rate ratio. Teacher sampling uses batches of 32, temperature 1 and top-p = 0 .95 .Student evaluation uses vLLM with the same temperature and nucleus cutoff, a 512-token output budget, and ignored end-of-sequence stopping. It adds neither presence/frequency penalties nor a marking processor. Seeds are 44, 142, and 256; each main seed has 200 evaluation responses. Appendix A specifies processing order and exceptions. 

Experimental comparisons. The eight codon-aware settings are position fractional/binary at strength 4, usage at strengths 2 and 4, and joint fractional/binary at strengths 4 and 8. This is a component-and-strength ablation, not a fully crossed search over every hyperparameter. The clean plain-KGW reference has three seeds, while its edited matrix contains only seed 44. The targeted-nucleotide campaign instead includes an unwatermarked negative control. We keep these baselines separate throughout. Extensions test different explanations for transfer. A 4B teacher with 100M or 500M students probes model size; 500 versus 2,000 training examples probes corpus budget. Five organism-conditioned campaigns test plain-KGW inheritance under different sequence sources. Fine-tuned-teacher controls test a second training stage. A keyed synonymous-codon transform changes the layer at which the mark is applied, while HyenaDNA students and a GROVER retokenization probe examine representation dependence. These experiments complement the main defense matrix rather than enlarging its matched-comparison denominator. 

Editing procedures. All tests here edit already generated student responses. CS performs frame-zero synonymous codon substitutions at probability 0.15. KT selects tokens from a corpus-derived bias dictionary and replaces them using a key-aware red-token fallback. KN replaces green tokens with red tokens at probability 0.5. DS uses a frequency-based discriminator to gate synonymous edits. These are local implementations, not equivalent reproductions of the original text paraphrasers or contrastive student-training attacks (Pan et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2025). A separate campaign targets the codon factors. Phase inserts one nucleotide after the first base of a selected codon at probability 0.30. Usage resamples a synonymous codon from T4 usage at probability 0.40. Combined applies Usage at 0.30 followed by Phase at 0.20. These operations do not consult the watermark key; their cleaned outputs are retokenized for auditing. Operation probabilities do not guarantee matched realized edit budgets, and the procedures are not detector-optimized attacks (Diaa et al., 2025). 5Preprint. 

Statistical unit and quality assays. For each seed we compute the fraction of responses above the same score cutoff and the mean response score, then average these seed summaries. Equal response counts make the average rate numerically equal to the pooled proportion, but do not make 600 responses independent training replicates. Error bars, where shown, are sample standard deviations across the three seed means. A mean score describes signal magnitude; the detection proportion describes the fraction of outputs crossing the threshold. We report both because an uneven score distribution can change them differently. Unwatermarked positive rates are empirical controls, not assumed to equal the nominal Gaussian tail. Quality is evaluated on cleaned DNA separately from detection. GC fraction and codon adapta-tion (Sharp & Li, 1987) summarize composition; overlapping 3-mer entropy and distinct 6-mer fraction summarize diversity. ORF coverage counts the union of complete forward-strand ATG-to-stop intervals of at least 60 bases across three frames. ESMFold (Lin et al., 2023) analyzes 150 index-paired translated outputs per condition, while BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990) searches 450 translated queries per condition against the E. coli K-12 proteome. Different selection procedures and denominators make these complementary assays, not interchangeable measures of function. Appendix E defines the metrics and reports the complete measurements.

# 6 DISCUSSION 

The experiments separate three properties often conflated in provenance evaluation: injection of bias into a teacher, learning of that bias by a student, and calibration of an attribution decision. The fixed-context calculation addresses the first; the clean and edited student outputs address the second. Neither alone establishes the third. A deployment would need held-out unwatermarked models and keys, a chosen decision unit, and thresholds calibrated to the relevant distribution. Repeated token contexts and shared evaluation prompts make extremely small Gaussian tail probabilities particularly difficult to interpret. The component ablation also clarifies the role of genomic structure. Position and usage factors change where bias is allocated, but do not act as biological validity constraints. Preferred-codon concentration can increase while ORF coverage and sequence diversity decrease. A quality-constrained choice should therefore specify the acceptable metric and compare configurations on that metric, rather than choosing the largest audit score alone. The current assay set does not measure expression, protein activity, or genomic function; low folding confidence is not evidence of non-inferiority. Three next comparisons would most directly resolve the remaining uncertainty: an edited three-seed plain-KGW reference at matched starting detection, detector-optimized transformations under realized sequence-quality budgets, and an independently calibrated audit across keys and tokenizer families. A teacher/student capacity theory would additionally require matched teacher scores and a crossed size-and-data design. These are distinct missing measurements, not implications of the present scale sweep or a fitted inheritance coefficient. 

# 7 CONCLUSION 

GenoTrace provides a codon-aware token-bias construction and demonstrates a signature learned through genome-model distillation. Joint fractional weighting produces strong clean signal and retains measurable detection under the tested token and nucleotide edits. Component, organism, scale, and training controls identify both alternative operating points and the shared-tokenizer scope of the result. Reading these findings together with the quality assays yields the central conclusion: genomic 9Preprint. structure can shape an inherited watermark, but detection strength, calibration, and biological utility must be evaluated separately. 10 Preprint. # A PROTOCOL AND IMPLEMENTATION 

This appendix specifies the sequence of operations behind the main experiments. Appendix B separates fixed-context mathematical statements from assumptions required for statistical testing. Appendix C gives the complete output-edit matrix; Appendix D gives organism, scale, training-control, and tokenizer results. Appendix E defines the computational quality assays and supplies their detailed measurements. The final coverage section identifies which quantities the supplementary export can reproduce. A.1 TRAINING AND GENERATION 

The default teacher and student are GenomeOcean-500M and GenomeOcean-100M. The defended training runs use 2,000 prompt–response examples, batch size 4, 4,000 optimization steps, and a maximum training length of 512 tokens. Real-genome examples replace 25% of the training corpus rather than increasing its size. The student objective is next-token prediction on sequences, not matching teacher probability vectors. LoRA uses rank 8 and scaling 16. AdamW uses learning rate 

2 × 10 −4 and weight decay 0.01; the schedule has 200 warm-up steps, cosine decay, and a minimum learning-rate ratio of 0.05. The three training seeds are 44, 142, and 256. Teacher sampling uses temperature 1 and top-p = 0 .95 with batches of 32. Student evaluation uses vLLM, temperature 1, top-p = 0 .95 , a 512-token output budget, and ignore_eos=True . It does not add a watermark logits processor or override presence/frequency penalties. Each main condition has 200 evaluation responses. Training and inference were run in Apptainer on NVIDIA H200 accelerators. Model parameters are not updated during auditing. A.2 DATA FLOW AND EXPERIMENTAL UNITS 

Prompts are genomic nucleotide prefixes rather than natural-language instructions. The main source is E. coli K-12 substrain MG1655, reference assembly GCF_000005845.2 . The main defense runs use the same training/evaluation prompt collections, truncated to their requested sizes. The three seed settings produce separate training runs; they are not three separately sampled reference genomes. The organism-conditioned studies change the sequence source, while the main usage factor uses the E. coli codon table. For each condition, the pipeline has six stages: 1. Generate teacher continuations from the training prompts using the selected position/usage factors and bias strength. 2. Replace the prescribed fraction of synthetic examples with real-genome fragments, keeping the requested corpus size unchanged. 3. Fit the LoRA student on the resulting sequence corpus with the recorded optimization recipe. 4. Merge the adapter into the student before the evaluation generation stage. 5. Generate evaluation continuations without a marking processor. Preserve response strings and model token IDs for separate uses. 6. Audit stored token transitions for the clean matrix, apply the specified output edits for transformed conditions, and compute quality measurements from cleaned DNA. Teacher training-example count, optimization steps, evaluation-response count, and valid translated-protein count describe different units. They should not be substituted for one another. The 512-token generation budget also does not imply a 512-nucleotide sequence because the vocabulary has variable-length tokens. Folding and homology require additional translation selection and therefore use their own denominators. A.3 GREEN LISTS AND READING FRAMES

CS decodes and cleans the DNA, attempts frame-zero synonymous substitutions at probability 0.15, and retokenizes the result. DS uses a logistic-regression discriminator over 64 token-frequency buckets. A 60-nucleotide local window gates synonymous editing at probability 0.30. It is a fixed post-processing procedure, not contrastive distillation or an optimized paraphraser. KT estimates positive bigram log-frequency ratios from watermarked and unwatermarked samples. A current token is selected when its stored score exceeds 0.5. The dictionary contains only scores above 0.1, while the low-score candidate branch requires a score below 0.1. Thus this dictionary makes that candidate set empty, and the implemented fallback draws a red token using the true key. KN replaces a green token with a random red token with probability 0.5. Both edit using the original preceding token, while the detector scores the resulting transitions. Neither enforces a nucleotide-distance or protein-preservation constraint. The data therefore support the named token-edit tests, not a claim of replicating the original attacks of Pan et al. (2025) or Yi et al. (2025). In the mechanism-targeted tests, Phase inserts a random base after the first nucleotide of a selected source codon. Usage draws from the synonymous choices weighted by T4 frequencies and may select the original codon. Combined applies Usage first, then Phase. Requested probabilities are 0.30 for Phase, 0.40 for Usage, and 0.30/0.20 for the two Combined components. These are not realized edit-rate guarantees. # F BROADER RESEARCH CONTEXT 

Detection, robustness, and attribution. Watermarking spans sampling-based constructions (Aaron-son, 2023), logit bias, semantic partitions, and cryptographic designs. Passive detection examines other signals, including probability curvature (Mitchell et al., 2023); stress-testing shows why de-tection results depend on the adversary and distribution (Sadasivan et al., 2025). Watermark design choices impose different trade-offs (Pang et al., 2024). Provenance is also distinct from training-data extraction (Carlini et al., 2021). Image-domain watermarks (Wen et al., 2023) further illustrate that robustness claims are tied to a representation and transformation class. We do not transfer their guarantees to genomic tokenization. 

Architectures and genomic representation. Transformer architectures (Vaswani et al., 2017) and their decoder implementations (Touvron et al., 2023) provide a foundation for genomic generators. DNABERT and DNABERT-2 (Ji et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024), the Nucleotide Transformer (Dalla-Torre et al., 2025), GROVER (Sanabria et al., 2024), and GenSLMs (Zvyagin et al., 2023) cover different tokenization and training choices. Geneformer and scGPT (Theodoris et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2024) address cellular representations rather than the DNA-generation setting considered here. A capacity or information-bottleneck interpretation (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017) is a hypothesis for future work, not an explanation established by our scale sweep. 32 Preprint. 

Biological utility and watermark layers. ProGen and ProteinMPNN study protein design and its experimental evaluation (Madani et al., 2023; Dauparas et al., 2022). Their results underline the distinction between computational similarity and demonstrated function. DNAMark, CentralMark, and FoldMark (Zhang et al., 2025; 2024) occupy sequence, translation, and structure-level watermark settings. Our study adds teacher-to-student transfer at the genome-token level. It does not claim superiority across these different tasks, nor does it infer wet-lab utility from codon statistics.
