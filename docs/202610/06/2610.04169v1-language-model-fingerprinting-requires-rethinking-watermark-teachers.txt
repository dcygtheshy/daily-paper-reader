ABSTRACT 

LLM fingerprinting via watermark distillation embeds a statistical watermark sig-nal into model weights, enabling model owners to identify their models behind black-box APIs. Revisiting a recent protocol, we find that its utility evaluation un-derstates text quality degradation in open-ended generation, favoring overly strong watermark teachers. Weakening the watermark improves text quality but sacrifices detectability. To move beyond this trade-off, we rethink whether text watermarks designed for verifying generated text are suitable distillation teachers for model fingerprinting. Such watermarks are typically designed to remain detectable from an individual output, limiting how sparse the watermark signal can be. In contrast, fingerprint verification can aggregate signal across queries, making sparser water-mark signals viable. This raises a key question: where should the sparse signal be placed? We analyze signal placement through token surprisal and show that, even at comparable watermark strength, different placements can target tokens with different plausibility under the base model. This motivates near-tie restriction, which uses top-1-relative logit gaps to restrict the watermark bias to tokens close to the base model’s top prediction. Across multiple models, near-tie improves detection–quality frontiers under deployment changes, preserves higher text qual-ity across query budgets, and further improves existing watermarking schemes when combined with them. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

The strong performance and widespread adoption of large language models (LLMs) have intensified the competition to develop increasingly capable models, with a growing number of such models released with open weights (Minaee et al., 2024; Qwen Team, 2025). Once released, however, these models may be deployed behind black-box APIs without honoring restrictive licenses, raising concerns about model ownership and unauthorized use (White et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024a). To build a healthy ecosystem, there is a growing need for reliable model provenance verification. Recently, black-box model fingerprinting methods (Xu et al., 2024a; Russinovich & Salem, 2024; Nasery et al., 2025b; Gloaguen et al., 2026) have been proposed for this purpose. They broadly fall into backdoor-based (Xu et al., 2024a; Russinovich & Salem, 2024; Nasery et al., 2025b) and watermark-based (Gloaguen et al., 2026) approaches. In this work, we focus on watermark-based fingerprinting, which uses a text watermark originally designed to verify the provenance of generated text (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023) as a distillation teacher. Its watermarked output distribution is distilled into model weights on a designated fingerprint domain, such as French, so that queries from this domain elicit a detectable statistical signal. Compared with backdoor-based approaches, this framework offers three advantages: ( i) statistical detection with false-positive control, ( ii ) natural-looking outputs that are challenging to detect and filter out, and ( iii ) robustness to deployment transformations such as decoding variations, quantization, pruning, and fine-tuning. Despite these advantages, we find that the utility evaluation of a recent protocol substantially un-derstates degradation in open-ended generation within the fingerprint domain. This favors overly strong watermark teachers that degrade generation quality for legitimate users in that domain. Re-ducing watermark strength improves text quality but sacrifices detectability, while more advanced watermarking schemes provide only limited gains when used as distillation teachers (Figure 3).

To move beyond this trade-off, we ask whether text watermarks are well suited as distillation teach-ers for model fingerprinting. Text watermarks (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Kuditipudi et al., 2023) are typically designed to remain detectable from an individual output, including short or modified outputs, which constrains how sparse their signal can be. Model fingerprinting, by contrast, can aggregate signal across mul-tiple queries, making sparser teacher signals viable. This raises a key question: where should the remaining signal be placed? We analyze signal placement through token surprisal and show that, even at comparable watermark strength, different placements target tokens of different plausibility. Motivated by this analysis, we propose near-tie restriction, which uses logit gaps to limit teacher modifications to tokens close to the model’s top prediction. Since it only restricts which tokens carry the watermark signal, near-tie can be incorporated into existing watermarking schemes with-out altering their underlying mechanisms. Empirically, this simple restriction substantially improves detection–quality frontiers across multiple models and watermarking schemes under common deployment transformations. It also yields higher generation quality across query budgets, and further improves existing watermarking schemes when combined with them. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We show that the evaluation of a recent watermark-based fingerprinting protocol under-states text quality degradation in open-ended generation and favors overly strong teachers. • We point out an overlooked implication of model fingerprinting: aggregating signal across queries makes sparser teacher signals viable. Motivated by token-surprisal analysis, we propose near-tie restriction using base-model logit gaps for signal placement. • Our comprehensive experiments show that near-tie restriction consistently improves detection–quality frontiers, preserves higher generation quality across query budgets, and further improves existing watermarking schemes when combined with them. 

2 RELATED WORK 

LLM fingerprinting. White-box fingerprinting methods (Refael et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025) provide robust verification but require access to model weights, limiting their practicality in real-world deployments. This motivates black-box methods, which verify ownership from queries and responses alone and broadly follow either a backdoor-based or watermark-based approach. Backdoor-based methods fine-tune a model to emit predetermined responses to secret trigger queries and verify ownership through exact matches (Xu et al., 2024a; Nasery et al., 2025b; Russinovich & Salem, 2024). However, atypical query–response pairs can undermine stealthiness, while exact-match verification can be fragile to deployment transformations. Watermark-based fin-gerprinting instead distills a watermark into model weights, enabling statistical verification from 2Preprint queries in a semantic domain and improving robustness to deployment transformations (Gloaguen et al., 2026). However, we find that its evaluation understates text quality degradation on open-ended generation within the fingerprint domain. We provide extended related work in Appendix A. 

LLM watermarking. LLM watermarking supports text provenance by embedding a hidden statis-tical signal into generated text. KGW (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), a widely used red–green scheme, partitions the vocabulary and biases green-token logits during decoding, making green tokens statis-tically overrepresented. Subsequent work develops alternative partitioning and biasing strategies to improve the detection–quality trade-off (Lee et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). Beyond decoding-time watermarking, Gu et al. (2024) show that watermarks can be distilled into open-weight models, enabling watermarked generation without modifying inference-time decoding. Building on this idea, Gloaguen et al. (2026) distill a semantically conditioned water-mark for model fingerprinting. However, this directly inherits teachers designed for text provenance detection, overlooking that fingerprint verification aggregates evidence across responses. We exploit this distinction to design a sparser teacher that better preserves text quality.

5 RETHINKING WATERMARKED TEACHERS FOR MODEL FINGERPRINTING 

In this section, we first highlight an overlooked implication of the distinction between watermarking for text provenance and watermarking for model ownership verification: the latter can rely on a sparse watermark signal (Section 5.1). Guided by this distinction, we analyze where this sparse signal should be placed in the teacher distribution (Section 5.2) and use the resulting insight to introduce near-tie restriction (Section 5.3). 5.1 FINGERPRINT VERIFICATION ALLOWS SPARSE WATERMARK SIGNALS 

Watermarking for text provenance typically requires detection from a single output, including short texts. In contrast, watermarking for model ownership verification can issue multiple queries to a suspect model and use aggregate responses to verify the ownership. This allows weaker watermark signals to accumulate across queries, making sparse signal placement viable; this in turn can reduce distortion of the base distribution. However, existing watermark distillation methods directly adopt a decoding-time KGW teacher, which biases every green token. Distilling these dense biases transfers modifications over a broad set of tokens to the student, which causes model degradation in Table 1 and Figure 2. This mismatch motivates restricting the green tokens biased by the watermark teacher. 5.2 WHERE SHOULD THE SPARSE SIGNAL BE PLACED ?To exploit the watermark sparsity allowed in fingerprint verification, we apply the watermark bias to only a subset of the green list. There is a key question of interest: which green tokens should carry the signal? Formally, at decoding step t, let S ⊆ Gt denote the biased subset, yielding

Compatibility with existing watermarks. We consider two representative KGW extensions that improve KGW’s detection–quality trade-off through complementary mechanisms. SWEET (Lee et al., 2024) applies the green-token bias only at high-entropy positions, where multiple plausible alternatives make the modification less disruptive. MorphMark (Wang et al., 2025b) instead adapts the bias strength to the cumulative base-model probability of the green list, avoiding a fixed modifi-cation across contexts. Both target provenance detection from a single, potentially modified output, where excessive token-level sparsity may provide insufficient evidence. Neither, however, deter-mines which individual green tokens to boost based on their top-1-relative logit gaps. Near-tie is complementary to these mechanisms and can be applied by further restricting their biased green-token sets, yielding SWEET-NT and MorphMark-NT. Implementation details are in Appendix G and empirical results are in Figure 6.

Detection–quality trade-off. Figure 3 shows that KGW-NT consistently improves the Pareto fron-tier, achieving higher worst-case detectability at comparable text quality or better text quality. This trend holds across model families and is consistent between PPL and LLM-judge evaluation. Al-though advanced methods such as SWEET and MorphMark partially improve upon KGW, restrict-ing KGW with near-tie selection yields a stronger frontier. KGW-NT also outperforms KGW-TK, which induces sparsity by biasing green tokens within a fixed top-k set. This comparison suggests that the gain arises from logit-gap-based selection rather than sparsity alone, as a rank cutoff can still include tokens separated from the top-1 prediction by large logit gaps.

7 CONCLUSION 

We show that a recent watermark-based fingerprinting protocol understates text quality degradation in open-ended generation and argue that watermark teachers should be designed for model veri-fication rather than inherit objectives from text watermarking. Aggregating signal across queries permits sparser signals, but placement also matters: different placements can target tokens of dif-ferent plausibility. Near-tie restricts watermark modifications to tokens close to the model’s top prediction, improving the detection–quality trade-off even when combined with existing schemes. This highlights the value of tailoring watermark teachers to model fingerprinting.

A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Additional fingerprinting methods. LLM fingerprinting methods differ in both verifier access and whether the fingerprint is explicitly embedded. White-box methods require access to model parameters or internal representations (Refael et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), whereas black-box methods verify models using only queries and responses. Black-box methods can further be divided into those that exploit existing model-specific behaviors and those that deliberately modify the model to embed a verification signal. The former avoids fingerprint-induced utility degradation but can be vulnerable to model transformations, whereas the latter allows robustness to be considered during fingerprint design. 

Black-box fingerprinting without model modification. Targeted methods search for distinctive query–response pairs, often using adversarial optimization such as greedy coordinate gradient (Zou et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Hayase et al., 2024). Such approaches were first studied for vision models (Peng et al., 2022; Lukas et al., 2019; Shao et al., 2025b) and later adapted to LLMs (Gubri et al., 2024), with extensions improving robustness to model merging (Yamabe et al., 2024), fine-tuning (Tsai et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025), and system prompts (Jin et al., 2024). However, optimized queries can be atypical and vulnerable to filtering (Nasery et al., 2025a). Untargeted methods instead use natural queries (Gao et al., 2024; Pasquini et al., 2025), but can be vulnerable to transformations such as fine-tuning (Shao et al., 2025a). 

Black-box fingerprinting with model modification. Backdoor-based methods embed specific query– response associations, typically through supervised fine-tuning (Xu et al., 2024a; Russinovich & Salem, 2024; Nasery et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2025a), following ideas from backdoor attacks (Gu et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021). Early approaches use unnatural queries (Xu et al., 2024a) or random responses (Russinovich & Salem, 2024), while recent work uses more natural queries and carefully selected responses (Nasery et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2025a;b). Fingerprints can also be embedded through model merging (Xu et al., 2024b) or knowledge editing (Wang et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2025b; Yue et al., 2025). Watermark-based methods instead embed a statistical bias in the output distribution. Gloaguen et al. (2026) distill a text watermark (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024) into model weights, enabling statistical verification from black-box outputs. 

Additional watermarking methods. Following KGW (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), numerous wa-termarking methods have been proposed to improve the detection–quality trade-off. SWEET (Lee et al., 2024) applies watermark bias only at high-entropy positions, while MorphMark (Wang et al., 2025b) adapts the bias strength to the cumulative base-model probability of the green list rather than applying a fixed modification across contexts. Adaptive Text Watermark (Liu & Bu, 2024) similarly selects high-entropy positions and uses semantics-conditioned logit scaling to reduce dis-tortion. DiPmark (Wu et al., 2023) introduces distribution-preserving reweighting to improve text quality while retaining detectability, whereas WatME (Chen et al., 2024) exploits lexical redundancy to preserve suitable token choices during watermarking. SSG (Gu et al., 2026) replaces random vo-cabulary partitioning with logit-balanced partitions to strengthen watermark signals, particularly in low-entropy generation. SpARK (Hoang et al., 2026) sparsely selects watermarking positions using linguistic or pseudorandom anchors. Wouters (2023) formulate watermark design as a multi-objective optimization problem and derive Pareto-optimal schemes within their watermark class that selectively watermark positions according to their expected quality cost. Near-tie instead exploits fingerprint verification’s ability to aggregate signal across responses by re-stricting, within each position, which tokens receive the watermark bias based on their top-1-relative logit gaps. Since it only restricts the bias-receiving token set, near-tie is complementary to existing watermarking mechanisms and can be combined with them, as demonstrated in our experiments. 14 Preprint

Remark. Teacher-side biasing may include positions with a green top-1 token even when the top-1– top-2 gap exceeds τ . This follows directly from our token-level restriction: the top-1 token has zero excess surprisal and is therefore always within the near-tie set when it is green. At such positions, the bias reinforces the already top-ranked token without promoting a lower-ranked alternative. To examine whether these biases should instead be removed, we additionally apply a position-level gate that suppresses all teacher bias whenever the top-1–top-2 gap exceeds τ . We evaluate this variant on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct using two frontier configurations with δ = 4 and q ∈ { 30 , 50 }.For q = 30 , the z-score without deployment transformations decreases from 17 .30 to 12 .90 , while the PPL ratio increases from 1.02 to 1.18 . For q = 50 , the corresponding values change from 

31 .77 to 29 .66 and from 1.04 to 1.10 , respectively. These results suggest that green top-1 biases provide useful training signal for watermark distillation. During distillation, the student learns the teacher’s context-dependent token preferences; reinforcing an already preferred green token there-fore strengthens the watermark supervision without promoting a less plausible alternative. Detection, however, cannot selectively identify these additional biased positions without checking whether the top-1 token is green, which would make position selection key-dependent. G IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS FOR NEAR -T IE COMBINATIONS 

Near-tie is combined with each watermark by further restricting the scheme’s original biased green-token set to tokens satisfying the near-tie criterion, while otherwise preserving its original mecha-nism. 

SWEET-NT. SWEET applies the logit bias only at high-entropy positions and detects the watermark signal from those positions using the base model. To combine SWEET with near-tie, we further restrict its biased green-token set to near-tie tokens. For detection, we score only positions satisfying both the SWEET entropy criterion and the near-tie criterion. 

MorphMark-NT. MorphMark promotes green tokens at every position by transferring probability mass from red to green, with the transferred amount determined by the current green mass. To combine MorphMark with near-tie, we restrict the promoted green-token set to near-tie tokens while otherwise preserving its original mass-transfer mechanism. While MorphMark uses the standard KGW detector, M ORPH MARK -NT uses the near-tie detector, scoring only positions that satisfy the near-tie criterion. 

KGW-TK. KGW-TK restricts the KGW bias to green tokens among the top-k tokens under the base-model distribution. We include it as a control to distinguish the effect of simply restricting the biased token set from that of using top-1-relative logit gaps. For detection, we use the standard KGW detector: unlike near-tie, KGW-TK applies the watermark bias at every position and therefore does not introduce a separate eligibility criterion for which positions should be scored. 18 Preprint 1 2 4 6

J PROMPT FOR TEXT QUALITY JUDGMENT 

We use GPT-5 as an LLM judge to assess text quality in open-ended generation, focusing specifically on linguistic degradation. The rubric considers grammatical errors, disfluency, malformed words, and excessive repetition, rather than broader task-level properties such as creativity. We do not score creativity separately, since it is highly prompt-dependent and may reflect stylistic variation rather than watermark-induced linguistic degradation. LLM-as-a-Judge Prompt You are an expert evaluator of French-language generation quality. You will receive a single model-generated text intended to be written in French. Your task is to evaluate the overall quality of the generated text. 

Evaluate: 

• grammatical correctness (agreement, conjugation, syntax, word order) • fluency and naturalness • coherence and readability • vocabulary and idiomatic usage • absence of non-French, malformed/invented words, garbled text, or excessive rep-etition The text was generated with a fixed token budget and may therefore end mid-sentence. If the final sentence is unfinished because of truncation, ignore it and do not treat the truncation itself as a quality defect. 

Score the text from 1 to 10: 

1–2: very poor 3–4: poor 5–6: fair 7–8: good 9–10: excellent Use the full range and do not default to the middle of each band. 

Return JSON: 

{"score": <integer>, "reason": "brief English 1-sentence explanation" }
