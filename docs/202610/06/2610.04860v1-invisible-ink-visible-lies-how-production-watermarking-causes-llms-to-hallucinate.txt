## Abstract 

Text watermarking helps identify AI-generated content, but its effect on factual reliability remains underexplored. In this paper, we study watermarking hallu-cination : factual errors induced or amplified by watermarking even when the required evidence is present in the context and the unwatermarked model can answer correctly. Using a controlled retrieval-augmented generation setting, we compare unwatermarked and watermarked generations under the same context, query, and decoding configuration, and quantify their factual accuracy decrease. Across six representative watermarking methods, including KGW, SWEET, DiP-mark, GumbelSoft, Gumbel-Max, and SynthID watermarking, we consistently observe watermark-induced hallucination. Watermarked outputs can remain fluent while introducing factual errors. We attribute this failure mode to two mechanisms: 

① token perturbations in the current-step arising from method-specific reweight-ing or keyed sampling, and ② prefix-induced attention drift, which accumulates through autoregressive decoding and weakens later attention to the factual context. Motivated by this analysis, we propose two plug-in interventions at the token and attention levels that can be integrated into existing watermarking methods to improve factuality. At a matched TPR of 0.90 at 1% FPR, combining the two inter-ventions reduces factual errors by approximately 90% relative to watermark-only decoding while preserving fluency and comparable decoding efficiency. Overall, this work highlights factuality as a first-class criterion in watermark evaluation, alongside detectability and robustness, and calls for careful factuality validation before deploying watermarks in fact-critical applications. Code is available at 

https://github.com/Haocheng-NJUST/WM-Hallucination .

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly generate long-form text in settings where provenance and factual reliability both matter, including clinical notes, legal summaries, financial reports, and public-facing assistants [1–4]. Text watermarking offers a way to identify model-generated content by embedding a statistical signal into the decoding process [5–7]. Some watermarking methods explicitly reweight token probabilities, while others preserve the model distribution under prescribed randomization. In both cases, the realized watermark-conditioned token choices can alter generation trajectories and factual outcomes.

Existing watermark evaluations typically assess watermarked text from two perspectives: watermark effectiveness , measured by detectability and robustness, and output quality , measured by perplexity, semantic similarity, and readability [8–10]. These criteria are necessary for deployment, but they often ignore whether the watermarked output preserves facts supported by the prompt or retrieved context. Recently, Hastuti et al. [11] showed that watermarked medical text can remain fluent while changing a dosage, date, identifier, or legal citation. They show that factuality can degrade even when coherence-oriented quality measures remain acceptable. However, their study does not isolate the factual degradation caused by watermarking itself, explain why such errors arise during decoding, or propose a mitigation strategy. We refer to this failure mode as watermarking hallucination and study it in a controlled retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) setting [12, 13]. Although retrieval and grounding can reduce LLM hallucinations [14–16], watermarking can introduce factual errors even when the relevant facts are available in the supplied context and the same model answers correctly without watermarking. We quantify this effect by the net factual accuracy decrease, ∆fact = Acc clean − Acc wm , where 

Acc clean and Acc wm denote factual accuracy for paired unwatermarked and watermarked outputs, respectively, evaluated under matched conditions. Positive values indicate lower factual accuracy under watermarking. Our evaluation covers six watermarking methods: KGW [5], SWEET [17], DiPmark [18], GumbelSoft [19], SynthID-style watermarking [7], and Gumbel-Max [6]. As shown in Figure 1(a), factual accuracy decreases across the evaluated methods, with larger decreases generally observed at higher watermark detection rates. The KGW breakdown in Figure 1(b) further shows that numerical and temporal facts exhibit larger decreases than the other fact types considered. To investigate the cause of this degradation, we compare factual accuracy between watermarked and non-watermarked generation, and analyze output logits and internal attention patterns. We identify two coupled pathways. At the token level, distortionary methods can directly bias the decoder against a fact-consistent token. At the prefix level, earlier watermark-conditioned token choices can alter the generated prefix and generation trajectory, thereby reducing subsequent attention to fact-bearing context. This prefix-induced drift is more difficult to address and has broader consequences than a local token substitution, because even non-factual prefix changes can accumulate and increase the likelihood of later factual errors. Motivated by these findings, we introduce two fact-preserving interventions, which can be integrated as plug-in components into existing watermarking methods to improve the factual accuracy of water-marked text. Specifically, Fact-Preserving Token Intervention (FPTI) limits watermark-conditioned changes at factual positions, whereas Fact-Preserving Attention Intervention (FPAI) targets prefix-mediated attention loss by restoring attention toward fact-bearing context. At TPR@1%FPR = 0 .90 ,their joint application reduces ∆fact by around 90% relative to watermark-only decoding, substan-tially improving the trustworthiness of watermarked generation. Nevertheless, a residual gap remains under strong watermarking. This gap reflects a hard constraint: even when a method preserves the model distribution under its prescribed randomization, a realized watermark-conditioned trajectory can still diverge from the narrow path required by exact factual grounding. 2Overall, the paper makes three contributions. • We identify watermarking hallucination as a factual reliability failure mode distinct from surface-quality degradation and ordinary hallucination, and quantify watermark-induced factual loss under paired controlled RAG evaluation. • We attribute watermarking hallucination to two coupled decoding-level pathways: token-level perturbations, arising through systematic bias in distortionary methods, and prefix-induced attention interference, which weakens later reliance on factual context. • We propose two complementary interventions that protect factual token selection and restore attention to supporting evidence, reducing factual degradation under watermarking across multiple watermarking methods and model families. 

## 2 Problem Formulation 

2.1 Context-Grounded Generation 

We study factual generation under a context-grounded setting. Let x denote the user prompt together with the retrieved or provided factual context. The model generates an answer y1: T autoregressively:

In a factual task, some answer spans are expected to be supported by the context. We call these spans factual decision points . At such a point, the model must select a token that is consistent with the evidence in x, such as an entity name, date, number, identifier, or relation. In standard generation, factual errors may arise when the next-token distribution is not sufficiently anchored to the relevant evidence. This can happen because of missing parametric knowledge, conflict between model memory and the provided context, or gradual drift away from the evidence during generation [14–16]. In RAG-based generation, the supplied context is expected to reduce this uncertainty. Therefore, factuality depends not only on whether the final string looks fluent, but also on whether the relevant evidence remains active when each factual span is generated.

We focus on decoding-time watermarking, which modifies token selection during generation without changing the model parameters [5, 17–19]. Let ˜y1: T denote the sequence generated under watermark-ing. At step t, the model computes the token sampling scores zt(yt | x, ˜y<t ) over candidate tokens

Watermarking therefore affects the next token both by changing its current sampling score and by changing the preceding tokens on which that score depends.

As illustrated in Figure 2, watermarking can erode this margin through two coupled effects. First, earlier watermark-conditioned token choices become part of the prefix and shift the model’s later distribution. Second, the current watermarking rule can alter the effective selection scores of candidate tokens or the realized token selection, favoring a fluent but factually incorrect competitor over the evidence-supported token. 3.2 Empirical Evidence of the Two Channels 

The decomposition in Equation (7) suggests two channels through which watermarking affects factual generation. 

① Direct token-level perturbation. At the current decoding step, watermarking can affect token selection through method-specific reweighting or keyed sampling rules. Because the watermark signal is not explicitly fact-aware, a fact-consistent token may receive weaker support or lose the realized selection to a plausible but incorrect competitor. This channel explains local substitutions in names, dates, numbers, and identifiers while preserving fluency. 

② Prefix-induced attention interference. Prefix-induced drift captures the effect of conditioning on the watermarked prefix rather than the clean prefix. In context-grounded generation, we measure one observable form of this drift by tracking how much the current decoding step attends to the fact-bearing tokens in the retrieved context. Let F(x) ⊆ { 1, . . . , |x|} denote the positions of input-side context tokens that support the target fact, and let At(j) be the attention weight from the current decoding position t to context token j. For example, in Figure 2, F(x) contains the context tokens corresponding to “May 12”, because they provide the evidence for the next factual token. We define the factual attention coefficient as

## 4 Fact-Preserving Interventions 

Mechanism analysis identifies two sources of watermarking hallucination: current-step token pertur-bations induced by the watermarking rule and prefix-induced drift accumulated through autoregressive decoding. We therefore introduce two targeted interventions. Fact-Preserving Token Intervention (FPTI) neutralizes watermark perturbations on fact-bearing output tokens, while Fact-Preserving Attention Intervention (FPAI) compensates for the reduced attention to fact-bearing context. Both interventions are designed as plug-in components for decoding-time watermarking methods: FPTI operates on output logits or sampling scores, whereas FPAI requires access to attention logits during inference. 

4.1 Fact-Preserving Token Intervention 

FPTI targets the token-level perturbation term in Equation (7). Given the input context x, we construct a vocabulary-level factual token set Vfact ⊆ V , which contains tokens corresponding to entities, dates, numerical values, identifiers, and other fact-bearing spans in the context. In our implementation, these tokens are extracted automatically from the RAG context [20] using named-entity recognition [21] and deterministic pattern-based matching [22]. During decoding, FPTI removes the current-step watermark perturbation from factual tokens while leaving the watermarking rule unchanged elsewhere:

## 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup Data and task. We evaluate watermarking hallucination in a controlled RAG setting [12, 13], where each query is paired with a fixed context paragraph containing the target facts. The evaluation set is constructed from Wikipedia passages associated with Natural Questions [23], using named entity recognition [21] and rule-based filtering [22] to retain verifiable facts. Queries cover four fact categories: numerical facts, identifiers, temporal facts, and qualitative facts, with 250 samples per category and 1,000 paired generations in total for each experimental condition. 

Models and methods. Our main experiments use LLaMA 3.1 8B Instruct [24]. For cross-model vali-dation, we additionally evaluate Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [25] and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct [26]. We incorporate six representative decoding-time watermarking methods: KGW [5], SWEET [17], DiP-mark [18], GumbelSoft [19], SynthID-style watermarking [7], and Gumbel-Max watermarking [6]. They cover logit-bias, entropy-aware, distribution-preserving, sampling-based, and tournament-style designs. We compare the proposed FPTI, FPAI, and their joint application with two representative inference-time hallucination mitigation baselines, DoLa [27] and DeCoRe [28], under KGW water-marking. Our interventions differ in that FPTI operates on output logits and watermark perturbations, while FPAI further uses attention logits over the provided context. 

Evaluation. We report the net factual accuracy decrease ∆fact defined in Equation (5), with lower values indicating less watermark-induced factual degradation. Watermark detectability is measured primarily by TPR@1%FPR. Because fact-preserving interventions can alter watermark detectability, for each model, watermarking method, and intervention setting, we independently calibrate the water-mark strength to restore the same measured TPR@1%FPR operating point before comparing factual accuracy. 5.2 Performance of Proposed Fact-Preserving Interventions Main intervention results. We first evaluate whether the proposed interventions reduce water-marking hallucination across watermarking methods, model families, and matched detectability levels. Specifically, we compare standard watermarking with its FPTI and FPTI+FPAI variants, where FPTI removes current-step perturbations on fact-bearing tokens and FPAI further compensates for prefix-induced loss of factual attention. The results in Table 1 show a consistent reduction in 

∆fact from standard watermarking to FPTI and further to FPTI+FPAI. For example, under KGW on LLaMA3.1-8B at TPR@1%FPR = 0 .90 , ∆fact drops from 12 .9% to 2.4% with FPTI and further to 0.9% with FPTI+FPAI. Similar reductions appear across Mistral-7B and Qwen2.5-14B, indicating that the effect is not tied to one model family. Nevertheless, residual errors remain more visible under very high detectability, suggesting that strong watermarking can push the prefix away from the evidence before later factual tokens are locally protected or attention-compensated. The table also compares the intrinsic factuality cost of different watermarking rules under the same detectability target. The results show that watermarking methods differ substantially in how much factual degradation they introduce. For example, on LLaMA3.1-8B at TPR@1%FPR = 0 .90 ,SynthID-style watermarking has a much lower ∆fact than KGW or DiPmark before intervention. This suggests that more controlled or distribution-aware sampling rules may be less prone to factual drift than stronger logit-bias-style perturbations, although all methods still benefit from FPTI+FPAI.

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

This paper studies factual degradation caused by watermarking itself. We identify watermarking hallucination as a failure mode distinct from ordinary hallucination and surface-quality loss: water-marked text can remain fluent and detectable while changing a context-supported fact. Using the net factual accuracy decrease ∆fact , we show that this effect appears across watermarking meth-ods, fact types, and model families. We attribute it to two coupled sources: current-step token perturbations and prefix-induced attention interference. FPTI and FPAI validate this mechanism by protecting fact-bearing output tokens and restoring attention to factual context, substantially reducing watermark-induced errors. Our study also reveals the boundary of local repair. Under strong watermarking, residual errors remain. Early watermark-conditioned choices can push the prefix away from the supporting evidence before later factual decisions occur. This suggests that factuality should be treated as a watermark design constraint, not only a post-hoc metric. Future watermarks should place their signal where it least interferes with grounding, avoiding numerical values, identifiers, dates, citations, and prefix positions that affect later factual attention. Overall, watermarking should not be assumed harmless in fact-critical applications: factual reliability must be evaluated alongside detectability, robustness, and output quality.

The growing deployment of AI systems has brought increasing attention to their security, including model ownership protection and robustness against adversarial threats [29–33]. As AI-generated content becomes more widespread, tracing its provenance is another important concern. Text watermarking offers a means of identifying model-generated content, but its effects on factual reliability also require careful evaluation. We review the literature on LLM watermarking, factuality risks in watermarked text, context-grounded generation and evaluation, and decoding-time methods for improving factuality. 

LLM watermarking. LLM watermarking methods embed detectable signals into generated text by modifying decoding, constraining sampling, or preserving distributional properties while adding a hidden statistical structure [5–9]. KGW-style methods bias token sets directly, while methods such as DiPmark, SWEET, GumbelSoft, token-specific watermarking, and distribution-adaptive watermarking seek better quality and robustness trade-offs [18, 17, 19, 34, 35]. Robustness studies examine paraphrase attacks, watermark removal, watermark stealing, and adaptive evasion attacks [36– 39]. These approaches are usually evaluated through detectability, false positive rate, robustness to edits, and surface quality. Our work studies a different axis: whether the same decoding changes also harm factual reliability. 

Factuality risks in watermarked text generation. Hastuti et al. [11] show that coherence-oriented evaluation is insufficient for medical watermarked generation and introduce a GPT-based judging workflow for factuality-aware assessment. Their results suggest that factual degradation can be more severe than coherence loss, especially when watermarking shifts entity-level or domain-specific con-tent. Our work builds on this observation but differs in scope and goal. First, we study watermarking hallucination under a paired context-grounded setting, where the relevant evidence is explicitly avail-able and the unwatermarked model can answer correctly. Second, rather than measuring factuality as an absolute output property, we use ∆fact to measure the net factual accuracy decrease between paired unwatermarked and watermarked generations. Third, we go beyond evaluation by attributing the degradation to current-step token perturbations and prefix-induced attention interference, and by introducing fact-preserving interventions that target these mechanisms. 

Context-grounded factuality and RAG evaluation. Retrieval-augmented generation is widely used to reduce hallucination by conditioning generation on external evidence [12, 13]. However, the presence of relevant context does not by itself guarantee faithful output: models may ignore the supplied evidence, rely on parametric priors, misread retrieved passages, or drift away from the context during long-form generation [16, 15]. Recent work further studies faithful RAG through internal model signals, for example using sparse autoencoders to identify features associated with source-faithful and unfaithful generation [40]. Factuality evaluation methods therefore increasingly emphasize evidence-grounded verification rather than surface fluency alone. FActScore decomposes long-form answers into atomic facts and checks whether they are supported [41], while SAFE uses structured search and verification to evaluate long-form factuality [42]. Our evaluation follows the same evidence-grounded principle but applies it to a different causal question: given the same model, prompt, retrieved context, and sampling configuration, does watermarking itself introduce factual errors? This paired design separates watermark-induced factual degradation from ordinary model errors or retrieval failures. 

Hallucination and decoding-time factuality. Hallucination research identifies failures caused by missing knowledge, knowledge conflict, weak grounding, uncertainty, and inference-time dynam-ics [14–16, 43]. A complementary line of work studies the knowledge boundary of LLMs, where factual failures arise because models cannot reliably memorize, access, or apply certain types of knowledge [44]. Decoding interventions can improve or degrade factuality, as shown by methods such as ITI and DoLa [45, 27]. These methods treat hallucination as a general generation failure and aim to make the model more faithful. In contrast, watermarking hallucination is induced by a source-attribution mechanism inserted for detectability: watermark-conditioned token choices can alter the generated prefix and later weaken attention to factual evidence. This makes water-marking hallucination a distinct inference-time failure mode: the model may be capable of using the evidence under clean decoding, but the watermark shifts the generation trajectory away from that evidence. Our FPTI and FPAI interventions are therefore not general hallucination mitigators; they are watermark-aware probes and repairs designed to separate and reduce the two mechanisms responsible for watermark-induced factual errors. 15 B Experimental Details

B.2 RAG Evaluation Setup 

We construct a controlled retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) setting, where each query is paired with a fixed context paragraph containing multiple verifiable facts. The evaluation set is built from Wikipedia passages associated with Natural Questions. We apply named entity recognition (NER) together with rule-based filtering to identify candidate contexts, and retain those that contain at least three facts of the same type. The resulting contexts span diverse topics (e.g., legal, medical, and financial domains) and cover four categories of factual information. 16 Each evaluation sample is designed to target a specific category of facts. For each query, the model is required to extract and answer one or more target facts of the same type from the provided context within a single response. We evaluate four categories of factual information, covering different types of reasoning and repre-sentation: 

Numerical facts refer to quantitative values (e.g., “78 mg/dL”), which require precise numerical correctness and sensitivity to units. 

Identifier facts include named entities, codes, or symbolic references (e.g., “AAPL”, “Doe v. Smith”), where correctness depends on exact matching. 

Temporal facts correspond to dates and time expressions (e.g., “May 12”), which must be chronolog-ically accurate. 

Qualitative facts represent descriptive or evaluative information (e.g., “relatively minor”), which involves comparative or contextual interpretation. For each fact type, we construct 250 evaluation samples, resulting in 1,000 paired generations for each experimental condition and a balanced test set across factual categories. 

Data source and privacy. Our evaluation data are constructed from publicly available Wikipedia passages associated with Natural Questions. We do not collect new user data or private information. The dataset is used only for controlled factuality evaluation, and each item is derived from a public context paragraph paired with a synthetic or filtered query. Although the contexts cover diverse topics, the benchmark is not intended to represent all fact-critical domains, such as clinical, legal, or financial deployment settings. 

B.3 Net Factual Accuracy Decrease Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluate watermark-induced factual degradation using the paired metric defined in Equation (5). For each evaluation item, we compare the unwatermarked output with the corresponding watermarked output under the same context, query, sampling configuration, and random-seed schedule. We record both correct-to-incorrect and incorrect-to-correct transitions and compute ∆fact as their difference over the full evaluation set. The factuality evaluator checks whether the answer is supported by the provided context. For numeri-cal, temporal, and identifier facts, we use exact or normalized matching whenever possible, allowing harmless surface variations such as equivalent date formats or unit spellings. For qualitative facts, we use structured annotation followed by manual verification on sampled outputs. A watermarked answer is marked incorrect if it omits a required target fact, changes a context-supported value, or introduces a contradictory claim. This paired protocol differs from absolute factuality judging. It does not ask whether a model is generally factual across open-ended tasks. Instead, it measures the change in factual accuracy caused by watermarking while accounting for transitions in both directions. We do not rely on an LLM judge for the main reported factuality labels. Rule-based decisions are manually audited on sampled outputs to check whether normalization and matching rules correctly capture factual support. 

B.4 Attention Measurement and Detectability 

To compute the factual attention coefficient αt, we first identify fact-bearing context tokens associated with the target facts. We then aggregate attention mass assigned to those tokens across layers and heads at each decoding step. The resulting αt is averaged over generated samples within the same watermarking method and detectability regime. This measurement is not used as a standalone explanation of model reasoning; it is used as an observable signal whose causal relevance is tested through FPAI in the main text. Detectability is aligned by grouping runs into low-, mid-, and high-Z regimes when the detector reports a Z-score. This avoids comparing methods at the same nominal hyperparameter when the 17 actual watermark signal differs. For methods without a directly comparable Z-score, appendix plots use the method’s native strength axis.

## C Additional Analysis 

C.1 Additional Watermark Families 

We further evaluate Unbiased Watermark with δ-reweighting [46], TextSeal [47], and MorphMark [48] to cover additional watermark designs. For each method, we evaluate 1,000 paired generations covering four fact categories, with 250 examples per category. The watermark strength is calibrated to a measured TPR@1%FPR = 0 .90 .All three methods produce transitions in both directions, although the incorrect-to-correct transitions do not fully offset the correct-to-incorrect transitions. Unbiased Watermark and TextSeal yield relatively small net factual accuracy decreases of 3.6 and 3.9 percentage points, respectively, whereas MorphMark produces a substantially larger decrease of 12.1 points. Together with the results in Figure 1 and Table 1, these findings show that the magnitude of watermark-induced factual degradation depends on the watermarking design. 

C.2 Fluency Metrics and Factual Errors 

We examine whether token-level perplexity separates factually correct and incorrect tokens. For each generated token with a factual correctness label, we compute its perplexity under the base model. Figure 6 shows that incorrect factual tokens are not restricted to high-perplexity regions. Correct and incorrect tokens overlap substantially, including in the low-perplexity region highlighted by the inset. This analysis explains the example in Table 7. A wrong token can be easy for the language model to generate if it fits the local sentence pattern. Factuality depends on whether the token matches the supplied evidence, not only on whether the token is likely under the model. Watermark evaluation therefore needs paired factuality measures such as ∆fact in addition to detectability and fluency checks.

## D Broader Impact 

This work can contribute to a safer and more responsible deployment of LLM watermarking by revealing a factual reliability risk that is not captured by standard detectability, robustness, or fluency metrics. Our study does not argue against watermarking. Instead, it shows that watermark deployment in fact-critical applications should be accompanied by factuality evaluation and domain-specific validation. By identifying watermark-induced hallucination and proposing fact-preserving interventions, our study can help model providers, policymakers, and application developers evaluate watermarking systems more comprehensively before deploying them in fact-critical settings. The 20 proposed analysis may also encourage future watermark designs to preserve factual reliability while maintaining provenance and accountability benefits. This work may also have unintended negative impacts. The finding that watermarking can induce factual errors could be misinterpreted as evidence that watermarking is broadly unreliable, potentially weakening trust in legitimate provenance mechanisms. In addition, our failure-mode analysis may help adversarial users identify settings where watermarked generation is more vulnerable to factual errors, such as strong watermarking or long generations. These risks highlight the need to interpret our findings as a call for more careful watermark evaluation and deployment, rather than as a rejection of watermarking itself. 

## E Limitations 

Although our interventions reduce watermark-induced hallucinations, they do not completely elimi-nate factual degradation under strong watermarking or long generations, because a realized watermark-conditioned trajectory can still diverge from the evidence-supported trajectory. In addition, our evaluation is conducted in a controlled RAG setting with selected fact types, and may not cover all forms of factuality errors in open-ended long-form generation. Future work should study more diverse domains, longer outputs, human-validated factuality evaluation, and watermark objectives that explicitly incorporate factuality-preserving constraints. 21
