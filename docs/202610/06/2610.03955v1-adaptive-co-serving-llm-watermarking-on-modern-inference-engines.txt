## ABSTRACT 

Large language model (LLM) watermarking is important for ownership verifica-tion and intellectual property protection. However, existing approaches focus on algorithmic design while treating LLM inference engines as separate components. This separation often introduces auxiliary models or external tools, increasing latency and memory overhead while limiting the use of modern inference opti-mizations. As a result, a deployment gap remains: practical watermarking must preserve utility, detectability, and robustness while minimizing serving overhead. To bridge this gap, we propose SWIFT, a framework that co-designs LLM water-marking with modern inference infrastructure. SWIFT (i) integrates text gener-ation and watermark construction within a shared LLM backend to reduce re-computation, improve cache reuse, and simplify system complexity; (ii) uses instruction-guided candidate generation with semantic protection to preserve fac-tual consistency and meaning; (iii) executes watermarking concurrently with text generation through asynchronous co-serving and adaptive scheduling to reduce latency and improve resource utilization; and (iv) leverages vLLM optimizations to enhance serving efficiency. Extensive experiments across domains and tasks show that SWIFT achieves strong utility, detectability, robustness, and down-stream performance while substantially improving serving efficiency. Specifically, it achieves the highest utility score (4.87), 99.65% detection accuracy compared with 76.65% for the low-latency baseline SynthID, and up to 97.7% detection un-der watermark removal attacks, with 5.9× lower latency than the highest-utility baseline, SafeSeal. These results demonstrate the benefits of co-designing wa-termarking with inference infrastructure for practical LLM serving. Code and artifacts are available at: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/SWIFT-0597 .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

As large language models (LLMs) (OpenAI, 2026; Anthropic, 2026; Google, 2026) become widely deployed across domains, their outputs can be copied, transformed, or redistributed without evidence of origin. This provenance gap makes it difficult to verify whether text was generated by a model or service, especially after it leaves the provider. LLM watermarking (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; Dathathri et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024; Munyer et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023) provides a practical solution by embedding imperceptible but detectable watermark signals in generated text, supporting origin verification and ownership attribution. For watermarking to be practical in LLM services, however, it must integrate efficiently into the inference engines that generate these outputs at scale. Existing watermarking methods largely focus on watermark construction and detection algorithms (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; Kuditipudi et al., 2023; Dathathri et al., 2024; Yoo et al., 2024), while LLM inference engines have evolved independently to optimize latency, memory, and throughput (Li et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025). This separation creates two deployment gaps. First, watermarking is often implemented as an auxiliary pipeline, relying on separate modules or external natural language processing (NLP) tools such as spaCy (Honnibal et al., 2020), Stanza (Qi et al., 2020), and NLTK (Bird et al., 2009) for named entity recognition (NER) and part-of-speech (POS) tagging, rather than sharing an engine with text generation (Wouters, 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Huo et al., 2024; Huang & Wan, 2025). Such fragmentation increases latency, memory usage, arXiv:2610.03955v1 [cs.CR] 2 Oct 2026 Figure 1: SWIFT example. Multiple prompts are processed concurrently through a shared vLLM backend. For a Kyoto guide, SWIFT generates context-aware substitutions (e.g., “beautiful” →

“wonderful”) while preserving key information such as locations and times. Watermark construction runs asynchronously alongside text generation, enabling streaming with minimal latency overhead. and system complexity. Second, serving optimizations such as asynchronous generation, key-value cache (KV-cache) reuse, prefix sharing, and scheduling remain largely unexplored for watermarking. These gaps motivate a question: Can we design watermarking as part of LLM inference engines, rather than as an auxiliary pipeline? To address this question, we introduce SWIFT (Fig. 1), to our knowledge, the first high-utility Serving-aware Watermarking for Inference-Friendly Text gen-eration framework that co-designs watermarking with modern inference engines. Built on vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), a high-throughput LLM serving engine with efficient KV-cache management and request scheduling, SWIFT addresses the gaps through a unified co-serving architecture. First, it co-serves text generation and watermark construction on the same LLM, inference engine, and GPU, eliminating separate modules or NLP tools while reducing duplicated computation, mem-ory overhead, and system complexity. For watermark construction, the shared LLM performs instruction-guided candidate generation, identifying watermarkable words, protecting named en-tities and sensitive words, and generating context-aware substitutions under semantic and factual constraints. Second, SWIFT introduces an adaptive co-serving scheduler that dynamically admits watermark requests based on queue length and waiting time pressure, preventing backlogs while preserving resources for text generation. This unified design further enables both workloads to share serving optimizations, including asynchronous execution, prefix caching, continuous batching, structured decoding, KV-cache management, and chunked prefill, allowing watermark computation to overlap with ongoing generation while reducing redundant computation and resource overhead. Experiments across tasks and LLMs demonstrate that SWIFT improves watermark quality and serv-ing efficiency over baselines, including KGW (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), SynthID (Dathathri et al., 2024), and SafeSeal (Dang et al., 2026). It achieves a utility score of 4.87 and 99.65% detection ac-curacy with 0.905 seconds (s) latency, 5.9× lower latency than the highest-utility baseline. SWIFT retains up to 97.7% detection accuracy under removal attacks and generalizes to downstream tasks, including summarization, short reasoning, and technical medical text, without performance degra-dation. Qualitative analysis shows its substitutions better preserve factual consistency. Its key-conditioned detection reaches 99.7% with the correct key and near-zero detection with incorrect keys. From a serving perspective, SWIFT achieves high throughput of 528.7 tokens/s, while reduc-ing KV-cache usage and attaining a 96.8% prefix-cache hit rate. Overall, our approach combines high utility, robust detectability, and efficient serving, making LLM watermarking more practical. 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

LLM Watermarking. LLMs generate text autoregressively by decoding one token at a time. LLM watermarking modifies this process or generated text to embed imperceptible but detectable water-mark signals for provenance verification, with a generator embedding the watermark and a detector verifying its presence. Existing methods broadly fall into two categories. In-processing watermarks 

modify token selection during decoding (Zhao et al., 2024; Liu & Bu, 2024; Hu et al., 2024). In-tegrated into text generation, these methods naturally support streaming but may not fully exploit modern serving optimizations (Fernandez et al., 2025). Their per-token modifications may accumu-late utility degradation over long outputs. Post-processing watermarks modify generated text, often using auxiliary NLP tools or language models (LMs) (Topkara et al., 2006; Chang et al., 2024). They can better preserve utility by selecting watermark locations and substitutions under seman-tic and contextual constraints (Yang et al., 2022; Dang et al., 2026), but add latency and memory overhead and are less suited to streaming. These trade-offs suggest that watermarking performance depends on both the embedding algorithm and its integration with the underlying inference system. 

LLM Inference Engines and Serving Systems. Modern LLM inference engines improve serving efficiency through KV-cache management, continuous batching, and request scheduling (Miao et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024). As KV-cache memory grows with the number and length of active requests, it can limit serving concurrency (Jiang et al., 2026; Mitzenmacher & Shahout, 2025). vLLM (vLLM Team, 2026a) addresses this bottleneck with PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023), which manages KV states in fixed-size blocks to reduce memory fragmentation and improve utilization. It also sup-ports continuous batching, prefix caching, asynchronous generation, and structured decoding. Other systems optimize complementary serving components: Orca introduces iteration-level scheduling (Yu et al., 2022), SGLang improves cross-request KV reuse through RadixAttention (Zheng et al., 2024), and Sarathi-Serve uses chunked prefill to mitigate prefill-decode interference (Agrawal et al., 2024). DistServe and Splitwise disaggregate prefill and decode execution (Zhong et al., 2024; Patel et al., 2024), while others explore preemptive and dynamic request scheduling (Wu et al., 2023). Despite these advances, watermarking has largely evolved independently of inference engine de-sign. Most methods use frameworks such as Hugging Face Transformers (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; Munyer et al., 2024; Dang et al., 2026), with limited cache reuse, asynchronous co-execution, and adaptive scheduling. Existing vLLM integrations mainly target in-processing watermarking (Verma, 2025; vLLM Team, 2026b), while post-processing methods with serving optimizations remain un-derexplored. This gap limits watermarking from fully leveraging LLM inference mechanisms. 

Challenges of Practical Watermarking. Bridging this gap requires addressing two interconnected challenges. First, watermark construction is often implemented as a separate processing pipeline. In-processing methods introduce operations along the decoding path, whereas post-processing methods may rely on external NLP tools or auxiliary LMs. These designs increase computation and deploy-ment complexity. Second, watermarking introduces an additional workload that must coexist with latency-sensitive text generation. Static or uncoordinated execution can create watermark backlogs, compete for resources, and delay streaming outputs. Thus, practical watermarking is both an al-gorithmic and systems problem: it must preserve utility, detectability, and robustness while sharing inference resources efficiently. These challenges motivate SWIFT, which integrates watermark con-struction into a shared LLM backend and adaptively co-schedules it with ongoing text generation. 

Threat Model. We consider a proprietary LLM service provider where users can submit prompts and receive outputs via an API. A black-box adversary can query the API and observe outputs but cannot access model parameters, architecture, or internal states. The adversary seeks to weaken watermark detection while preserving the original content through watermark removal attacks such as paraphrasing, insertion, synonym substitution, and rewriting (Zhang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2021). 

## 3 SWIFT: A DAPTIVE CO-S ERVING LLM W ATERMARKING 

3.1 DESIGN OBJECTIVES AND SYSTEM OVERVIEW 

The design of SWIFT follows four objectives: (i) Unified design , minimizing external dependencies through a shared LLM backend; (ii) Utility preservation , maintaining semantic and factual consis-tency through instruction-guided watermarking; (iii) Adaptive co-serving , coordinating concurrent workloads to control watermark backlogs and maintain streaming responsiveness; and (iv) Serving efficiency , leveraging inference optimizations to reduce computation, memory, and latency. Fig. 2 and Alg. 1 (Appx. A) illustrate the architecture of SWIFT, which integrates text and water-mark generation streams within a single vLLM engine. As both streams share a backend, SWIFT loads the LLM once, enabling both workloads to leverage serving optimizations and reducing exter-nal dependencies. The workloads execute asynchronously, overlapping watermark processing with Figure 2: Overview of SWIFT. ongoing text generation, while an adaptive co-serving scheduler coordinates their execution under varying demand. Together, these components balance watermark quality, detectability, and serving efficiency. The following subsections describe co-serving, scheduling, and watermark construction. 3.2 ASYNCHRONOUS CO-S ERVING OF TEXT AND WATERMARK GENERATION 

SWIFT co-serves text generation and watermark construction as two asynchronous inference streams sharing the same vLLM engine, LLM, and GPU. Given a prompt x, the text generation stream autoregressively decodes a response y = ( y1, . . . , y T ) ( T > 0) , producing tokens incre-mentally rather than waiting for the complete response. This enables streaming generation while allowing watermark processing to begin before text generation finishes, reducing the latency of con-ventional generate-then-watermark pipelines (He et al., 2022a; Hao et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2022). By reusing the same inference backend for both workloads, SWIFT eliminates separate watermark-ing engines, external NLP tools, and auxiliary LMs, reducing complexity and memory overhead. To enable asynchronous execution, SWIFT maintains a segment buffer that collects generated to-kens and identifies completed segments (typically sentences). At decoding step t, token yt is ap-pended to the buffer. Upon detecting a sentence boundary, SWIFT extracts the completed segment 

si and submits it as a watermarking request to the shared LLM. Meanwhile, text generation continues decoding subsequent tokens yt+1 , y t+2 , . . . without waiting for watermarking to finish (Fig. 2). The adaptive co-serving scheduler coordinates watermark request admission alongside ongoing genera-tion to balance both workloads, as detailed in Section 3.3. Once processed, watermarked segments are streamed to users in their original order, maintaining incremental output delivery. Because both streams share the same LLM backend, SWIFT loads the model once and leverages vLLM serving optimizations to improve efficiency (vLLM Team, 2026a). Prefix caching reuses KV states for shared watermark instructions, while continuous batching incorporates newly admitted wa-termark requests. Request prioritization manages latency-sensitive workloads, and chunked prefill mitigates prefill-decode interference. Shared KV-cache management and consistent model precision further reduce memory overhead and avoid redundant model representations or conversion costs. 3.3 ADAPTIVE CO-S ERVING SCHEDULER 

To coordinate text generation and watermark construction, SWIFT introduces an adaptive co-serving scheduler that adjusts watermark request admission based on runtime workload pressure (Fig. 3). The inference engine co-serves text generation and watermark requests, each occupying a sequence in the mixed serving batch ( mixed-batch ). Rather than reserving a fixed portion of the batch for watermarking, the scheduler adapts watermark admission based on queue length and wait-ing time, balancing workloads while maintaining streaming responsiveness and resource efficiency.

To reduce complexity and external dependencies, SWIFT reuses the same LLM for candidate gen-eration and employs key-conditioned selection to embed detectable watermarks while preserving semantic and factual consistency. We next describe these mechanisms and watermark detection. 

Instruction-guided Candidate Generation. To preserve output utility without auxiliary NLP tools or LMs, SWIFT uses instruction-guided candidate generation to identify watermarkable words and generate substitutions using the same LLM. A key insight is that replacing words with context-aware synonyms can preserve meaning while protecting key information (e.g., named entities or sensitive spans) to ensure factual accuracy. Accordingly, the instructions guide the LLM to identify protected information, replaceable words, and generate substitutions under semantic and factual constraints (Fig. 6). Once admitted by the adaptive scheduler, each completed segment 

si = ( wi, 1, w i, 2, . . . , w i,n i ), where wi,j is its j-th word, is processed through this pipeline. The LLM identifies watermarkable positions Ii ⊆ { 1, . . . , n i}, typically replaceable nouns, verbs, ad-jectives, and adverbs that can be substituted without altering the meaning or factual content of si.For each segment si, SWIFT constructs a watermarking prompt Pi = [ Pshared ; si], where Pshared is a shared instruction prefix and the completed segment si is the segment-specific suffix. The shared prefix specifies protected information rules, semantic and factual constraints, candidate-generation instructions, and few-shot examples (Appx. C). Since Pshared is identical across watermark requests, vLLM prefix caching reuses its KV states, reducing repeated prefill computation. This is particularly beneficial because the shared prefix is substantially longer than the segment-specific suffix. Watermark requests are processed via vLLM continuous batching (vLLM Team, 2026a), joining the shared serving batch as slots become available. This enables dynamic scheduling and concurrent execution of text generation and watermark requests with varying sequence lengths (Yu et al., 2022). To ensure consistent candidate generation, SWIFT leverages vLLM structured output decoding to constrain responses to a JSON schema. For each watermarkable position j ∈ I i, let Ci,j denote the set of context-aware candidate substitutions for wi,j (e.g., {“beautiful”: [“wonderful”, “scenic”, “magnificent”] }). The structured output for segment si is represented as Ci = {wi,j : Ci,j | j ∈ I i}.This reduces malformed outputs and provides consistent candidate mapping for watermark selection.

Watermark Detection. SWIFT verifies watermarked text using a key-conditioned contrastive de-tector, which determines whether a text is watermarked under a specific secret key (Dang et al., 2026). Given a text y and key k, the detector predicts D(y, k ) ∈ { 0, 1}, where D(y, k ) = 1 indicates that y is watermarked under k, and D(y, k ) = 0 otherwise. The detector encodes y with a bidirec-tional Transformer encoder (RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019)), maps k to a deterministic key embedding, and combines the text and key representations (element-wise interaction and absolute difference) for classification. During training, matched text-key pairs are positives, while mismatched ones are neg-atives. The detector is optimized with a detection loss for binary classification and a contrastive loss that aligns matched pairs while separating mismatched ones, enabling provider-specific detection.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Our extensive evaluation covers (1) watermark effectiveness and efficiency in terms of utility, de-tectability, and latency compared with representative baselines; (2) robustness against watermark removal attacks; (3) generalization across domains, tasks, and LLMs; (4) adaptive scheduling anal-ysis; (5) key-conditioned watermark detection; and (6) qualitative analysis and ablation studies. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS 

Baselines. We compare against representative LLM watermarks: KGW (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), a logit-bias method that steers decoding toward preferred tokens; SynthID (Dathathri et al., 2024), a sampling-based method using pseudorandom keys; and SafeSeal (Dang et al., 2026), a lexical sub-stitution method with named-entity protection. These baselines cover two watermarking categories. 

Watermark Removal Attacks. We evaluate robustness against three attacks: Dipper , a sentence-level paraphrasing attack (Krishna et al., 2024); Substitution , a token-level synonym substitution (Pan et al., 2024); and SIRA , an LLM-based self-information rewriting attack (Cheng et al., 2025). 

Tasks, Datasets, and LLMs. We evaluate SWIFT across four tasks: text generation on C4 dataset (Dodge et al., 2021), summarization on CNN/Daily Mail dataset (Hermann et al., 2015), mathemat-ical reasoning and short text watermarking on GSM8K dataset (Cobbe et al., 2021), and technical medical question answering on MedMCQA dataset (Pal et al., 2022). For C4, we use the first 50 

tokens as prompts and generate up to 200 tokens. Other datasets follow their respective summa-rization or question-answering formats. Gemma-3-27B-IT (Team et al., 2025) serves as the shared LLM, while Qwen2.5-32B (Yang et al., 2024), LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and Ministral-8B-Instruct (AI, 2024) support cross-LLM and key-conditioned detection experiments. Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate SWIFT along four key dimensions: utility, detectability, ro-bustness, and serving efficiency. Utility measures preservation of semantic meaning and factual consistency, assessed by GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026) as an LLM-as-a-judge on a 0-5 scale. Detectabil-ity measures the detector’s ability to distinguish watermarked from non-watermarked outputs using balanced detection accuracy averaged across both output classes. Robustness measures detection accuracy after watermark removal attacks, reported as the fraction of attacked watermarked outputs correctly classified as watermarked. Serving efficiency is measured primarily by end-to-end latency.

4.2 EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 

Watermark Effectiveness and Efficiency. Fig. 4 demonstrates that SWIFT achieves the best over-all trade-off among utility, detectability, and serving latency. On text generation , SWIFT attains the highest utility (4.87) and detection accuracy (99.65%) with only 0.905s latency. KGW and SynthID are slightly faster (0.88-0.90s) but achieve substantially lower utility (2.01-2.02) and detection accu-racy (76.65-95.50%). SafeSeal achieves strong watermark quality but incurs 5.34s latency, making SWIFT approximately 5.9× faster. On text summarization (Fig. 4(b)), SWIFT similarly achieves the highest utility (4.57) and detection accuracy (98.8%) with only 0.559s latency. Although KGW and SynthID are slightly faster, their detection accuracies drop to 67.35% and 59.25%, respectively. SafeSeal reaches 96.45% detection but with lower utility and approximately 2× higher latency. Sim-ilar trends are observed on Qwen for both text generation and summarization (Fig. 7), demonstrating consistent performance across LLMs. These results highlight the benefit of co-designing watermark-ing with inference infrastructure. Instruction-guided candidate generation preserves semantic and factual quality, while key-conditioned selection provides strong detectability. Meanwhile, asyn-chronous co-serving, adaptive scheduling, and vLLM optimizations reduce watermarking overhead, allowing SWIFT to approach the latency of in-processing methods while substantially improving utility and detectability, and to remain consistently much faster than post-processing SafeSeal. 

Robustness Against Attacks. Fig. 4(c) shows that SWIFT remains highly robust to watermark re-moval attacks, retaining 97.7% detection rate under Substitution and 61.3% under Dipper. SWIFT and SafeSeal substantially outperform KGW and SynthID. This robustness is partly enabled by the key-conditioned detector, which learns watermark patterns from text-key pairs rather than prede-fined rules or statistical scores that may be disrupted by attacks. SIRA reduces SWIFT’s detection rate to 26.4%, but at substantial cost. Its attacked outputs achieve only a 0.145 pairwise win rate against the original watermarked outputs using the same LLM judge, indicating considerable utility degradation. SIRA also requires 8.933s, about 9.87 × SWIFT’s 0.905s latency. Therefore, while SIRA weakens detectability, it incurs substantial utility loss and runtime overhead.

Beyond general text generation and summarization, practical watermarking should remain effective on short outputs and technical domains where constrained token choices can reduce watermark capacity. On short mathematical reasoning (Table 1), SWIFT preserves the original task accuracy (without watermark) of 93.2%, achieves the highest utility (4.93), 90.70% detection accuracy, and the lowest watermarking latency (0.352s). KGW and Syn-thID slightly reduce task accuracy and achieve only 53.35% and 54.95% detection, respectively. Adaptive Co-serving Scheduler. Fig. 5(a) compares SWIFT’s adaptive scheduler with fixed ad-mission ratios in [0 .1, 0.95] . The adaptive scheduler updates every five scheduling steps and achieves the lowest latency of 0.905s. Low fixed ratios allocate insufficient capacity to watermarking, causing request backlogs and higher latency, while an overly high ratio ( r = 0 .95 ) interferes with text gen-eration and increases latency. In contrast, SWIFT adapts watermark admission to runtime workload pressure, balancing both streams while maintaining low latency and streaming responsiveness. 

Key-conditioned Detection. Fig. 5(b) evaluates SWIFT’s universal detector across LLMs using correct and incorrect keys. With the correct key, detection remains high at 99.2-99.7% across Gemma-3, Qwen-2.5, and LLaMA-3.1, while incorrect-key detection remains near zero (0-1.9%), demonstrating strong key specificity and reducing false attribution. The key-conditioned objective aligns matched text-key pairs while separating mismatched pairs, enabling key-specific verification. On the unseen Ministral model, only 3.7% of outputs are detected using the Gemma-3 key, further indicating that the detector does not associate unseen models with known keys. A single detector can therefore generalize across LLMs without per-model retraining, simplifying system integration.

Limitations. Despite its effectiveness, SWIFT has several limitations. First, watermark qual-ity depends on the capability of the shared LLM . Since the same model performs text generation, watermarkable-word identification, and candidate substitution, weaker models may produce less reliable substitutions or fail to preserve protected information, reducing output quality and utility in specialized domains. Second, SWIFT does not fully leverage KV-cache reuse . While shared instructions benefit from prefix caching, reusing KV states for segment-specific suffixes remains non-trivial because text generation uses the original prompt and history, while candidate genera-tion uses a separate watermarking context. Finally, although SWIFT achieves competitive serving efficiency (Table 12), it currently streams outputs at the sentence level rather than token by token. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented SWIFT, a serving-aware LLM watermarking framework that co-designs watermark construction with modern inference infrastructure. SWIFT unifies text generation and watermark construction within a shared LLM backend, reducing reliance on auxiliary models and external NLP tools, while instruction-guided candidate generation and entity protection preserve semantic and factual utility. Its asynchronous co-serving architecture and adaptive scheduler coordinate text generation and watermark workloads under varying demand while leveraging inference-engine op-timizations. Experiments across domains, tasks, and LLMs demonstrate strong utility, detectability, robustness, downstream task preservation, and serving efficiency. These results highlight the bene-fits of jointly considering watermark quality and inference-system design. Future work will explore finer-grained streaming through adaptive token release and improved KV-cache reuse across gener-ation and watermarking contexts, further improving responsiveness and computational efficiency. System-level Optimizations. Beyond the scheduling mechanism and serving techniques, SWIFT incorporates additional system-level optimizations for efficient shared inference. Both text gener-ation and watermark construction use the same LLM with a unified precision bf16 configuration, avoiding duplicate model weights, separate runtimes, and additional memory overhead. SWIFT also leverages chunked prefill (vLLM Team, 2026a), which interleaves long watermark-prefill com-putation with ongoing decoding to reduce interference with latency-sensitive generation (Agrawal et al., 2023). Together, these optimizations improve resource efficiency under concurrent workloads.

Additional Evaluation Setup. Unless otherwise specified, all results are computed over 1, 000 

samples. In the C4 text generation, we use the first 50 tokens of each C4 sample as the prompt and generate up to 200 output tokens. In the CNN/Daily Mail Summarization, we use a fixed few-shot summarization prompt and instruct the model to generate a summary of at most 60 words. In the GSM8K reasoning task, the model is prompted to generate a reasoning trace of at most 50 tokens followed by a final answer. Task accuracy is computed by comparing the extracted final answer with the ground-truth answer. In the MedMCQA, we use the same reasoning-and-final-answer format as GSM8K but allow explanations of up to 100 tokens. We retain only single-answer questions with a valid correct option and a non-empty explanation longer than 50 words to ensure sufficient text for watermarking and evaluation. For watermark detection, we train the RoBERTa-large (356M) (Liu et al., 2019) key-conditioned detector once on 20 , 000 C4 samples and evaluate it on 1, 000 held-out samples. Training and evaluation prompts come from the official C4 training and validation splits, ensuring no overlap. The same detector is reused without retraining for summarization, mathematical reasoning, and medical QA, enabling cross-domain and cross-task evaluation under distribution shift. For utility evaluation, we use GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026) as an LLM-as-a-judge to evaluate semantic and factual preservation between the original and watermarked outputs using the fixed prompt shown in Fig. 10. The reported utility is the average score over all evaluated samples. For serving evaluation, each method is evaluated over 1, 000 watermarked requests under the same workload. Throughput, GPU KV-cache usage, and prefix-cache hit rate are collected from standard vLLM runtime statistics with log statistics enabled, while TTFT and TPOT are measured using the standard vLLM benchmark ( vllm bench serve ). For asynchronous co-serving systems such as SWIFT, these engine-level metrics may reflect both text and watermark workloads; we therefore treat throughput and E2E TTFT mainly as supplementary serving metrics.
