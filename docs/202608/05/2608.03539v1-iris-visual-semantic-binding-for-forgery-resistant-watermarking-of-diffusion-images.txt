Abstract 

Most in-generation diffusion watermarks embed patterns in-dependent of the image that carries them, and attackers trans-plant the marks onto images the generator did not produce, resulting in forgery. Binding the mark to visual semantics pre-vents such transplantation, yet existing bindings anchor to a proxy image rather than the image they mark. Realizing visual-semantic binding inside generation faces two challenges. The mark derives from the image itself yet enters the sampling trajectory before that image exists, and may itself shift the semantics it binds. The binding also meets opposite sensitiv-ity demands, breaking under semantic change while holding through common processing. We present IRIS , a training-free watermarking scheme that embeds an Intrinsic Ring Identifier from Semantics. IRIS reads a content code from the non-watermarked generated image, derives a one-time ring from the code and a secret key, returns to the final low-noise steps of the same trajectory and blends the ring in, after the semantics it binds are settled. To meet the opposite sensitivity demands, the code is read through a canonicalization shared between em-bedding and detection, holding through common distortions and mild regeneration while flipping under semantic change. Detection recomputes the ring from the query image and the key alone, and the mark therefore fails on a foreign or spliced image, with acceptance tracking semantic displacement. On three prompt datasets IRIS detects reliably and stays close to its same-seed non-watermarked counterpart, a fidelity prior in-generation marks do not reach. While forgeries transfer fixed-pattern marks and regeneration strips post-hoc marks, IRIS alone among the compared marks withstands both. 

# 1 Introduction 

Latent diffusion (Rombach et al. 2022) underlies most large-scale AI image generation, powering Stable Diffusion and DALL-E 3 (Betker et al. 2023), and flow models such as FLUX inherit its latent space (Labs et al. 2025). Diffusion images are hard for people and forensic detectors to tell from real images (Lu et al. 2023), and the unsafe imagery these models return (Qu et al. 2023) makes tracing images to their source a pressing concern for platforms, regulators, and the public (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity 2026; European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024). Watermarking has become a necessary re-sponse. Prevailing schemes embed a pattern into the latent along the DDIM trajectory, weaving the mark into the im-age (Wen et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2024b). Yet these marks are forgeable without the key (Yang et al. 2024a; Müller et al. 2025). Attackers can distill a mark by averaging wa-termarked examples and stamp it onto chosen content (Yang et al. 2024a), or imprint one from a single reference image without recovering the pattern (Müller et al. 2025). Binding the mark to visual semantics promises to make the mark forgery-resistant by requiring verification to match the hidden mark against the visual semantics of the image, and the mark thereby cannot be transplanted to images it does not match. Binding the in-generation mark to visual seman-tics for diffusion images faces two challenges. First, it faces 

causal entanglement . The mark depends on the semantics of the image it binds to, yet it is injected mid-sampling before that image exists, and the perturbation it introduces may it-self alter those semantics as it propagates along the trajectory, invalidating its own derivation. Second, it faces opposite sen-sitivity requirements for semantic and non-semantic changes. Verification should hold under common distortions such as JPEG compression and fail once the visual semantics change. This failure is what flags a forged or altered image. The closest semantic binding, SEAL (Arabi et al. 2025), sidesteps the causal entanglement rather than solving it. It hashes the caption embedding of a pre-generated proxy im-age with SimHash, regenerates the delivered image from an initial noise latent constructed to carry the hash bits, and the mark is thereby bound to the proxy’s caption rather than to the delivered image itself. Captions are a coarse summary, and images sharing one caption can share the mark. SEAL thereby remains vulnerable to mark transplantation. We present IRIS , a training-free in-generation watermark-ing scheme that embeds an Intrinsic Ring Identifier from the Semantics of the delivered image. To address the causal entanglement, IRIS derives a phase ring from the CLIP em-bedding of the clean image at the end of the sampling tra-jectory and blends the ring back into the trajectory’s later DDIM steps at an energy matched to the latent’s own spec-trum, leaving the visual semantics unchanged and letting the image itself carry the mark. To address the opposite sensitiv-ity requirements, IRIS reads the CLIP embedding through a canonicalization pipeline at both embedding and verification, keeping the reading stable under common distortions such as JPEG compression, and derives the mark from a keyed hash of the embedding, making it sensitive to semantic changes. Measured along the CLIP image-embedding angle, IRIS’s arXiv:2608.03539v1 [cs.CV] 4 Aug 2026 acceptance falls as visual displacement grows, whereas a caption-level binding stays open to targeted edits that leave the caption unchanged. Our contributions are threefold. 

• We identify two challenges in binding the in-generation mark to visual semantics for diffusion images, causal en-tanglement and opposite sensitivity requirements. 

• We propose IRIS, a training-free watermark whose one-time ring is derived from the trajectory’s endpoint by a keyed hash of a canonicalized CLIP embedding, blended back at an energy matched to the latent’s spectrum. 

• We evaluate IRIS on three prompt datasets, where it achieves detectability, fidelity, and security at once. It preserves the generated content at PSNR 30 .2 while in-generation baselines redraw it at 14 .4 or below, and holds the lowest attack success rate averaged over the four at-tacks. We trace the retention decay under semantic dis-placement to the code recomputed at verification, which drifts and no longer derives the embedded ring. 

# 2 Related Work 

In-Generation Watermarks for Diffusion Images. Wa-termarking for diffusion images can be divided into post-hoc marking and in-generation marking. Post-hoc marks (Fer-nandez et al. 2023; Cox et al. 2007) preserve the image content, but they sit shallow and are easily removed by add-noise-then-denoise regeneration. In-generation marks inject a pattern into a latent along the sampling trajectory and read it back by inversion, making them withstand regeneration re-moval (Zhao et al. 2024). Tree-Ring (Wen et al. 2023) writes a ring into the Fourier spectrum of the initial noise, and RingID (Ci et al. 2024) extends it with multi-channel keys. Gaussian Shading (Yang et al. 2024b) maps a secret message to a Gaussian-preserving latent. PRC-Watermark (Gunn, Zhao, and Song 2025) seeds the initial noise from pseudoran-dom codes. ROBIN (Huang, Wu, and Wang 2024) relocates the mark to an intermediate latent with a hidden guiding prompt. However, these patterns are independent of their car-rier images, enabling forgeries which make attacker-chosen content read as watermarked. Müller et al. (2025) invert a watermarked image and regenerate attacker-chosen content from the recovered latent, carrying the mark into the new image. Saberi et al. (2024) query the system with a noise image and transfer the resulting mark to a natural image. 

Content-Bound Watermarks. These forgeries exploit the same weakness, a mark that does not depend on the im-age carrying it. Content-bound watermarks counter them by binding the mark to its carrier image. Existing designs differ in when they embed the mark and where they anchor this binding. SEAL (Arabi et al. 2025), SemBind (Zhang et al. 2026), and SLICE (Gao et al. 2026) derive their bindings from prompt-conditioned auxiliary images rather than the de-livered image, requiring an additional full generation. SEAL hashes the auxiliary image’s caption embedding. SemBind trains a private semantic masker to assign similar codes to images from the same prompt. SLICE uses a VLM to extract four semantic descriptors from the auxiliary image. SafeTR and Dual-Guard instead embed during generation but rely on per-image records. SafeTR links a content-independent latent index to a prompt stored in an external record (Xie et al. 2026b). Dual-Guard writes a registered fingerprint into the initial noise and the final latent and stores a reference latent in its main setting (Xie et al. 2026a). MetaSeal derives a cryptographic signature from the completed image and em-beds it post hoc with an invertible neural network (Zhou et al. 2026). IRIS derives its binding in place from the delivered image’s visual semantics, along the trajectory that produces it, without watermark-specific training, an auxiliary genera-tion, per-image records, or a post-hoc embedding pass, and its verification is therefore blind and stateless. 

# 3 Preliminaries 

Latent diffusion and DDIM inversion. A latent diffusion model generates an image by denoising in the compressed latent space of a VAE (Rombach et al. 2022). Given a text prompt p and an initial Gaussian latent zT , the model’s de-noising network predicts the noise at each step, and a sampler uses these predictions to produce a sequence of T progres-sively cleaner latents ending at z0. The model’s VAE decoder 

D then maps z0 to an image x = D(z0). DDIM is a deter-ministic sampler that introduces no additional random noise between denoising steps. A fixed prompt p and initial latent 

zT therefore determine the denoising trajectory. DDIM inversion applies the DDIM updates in the opposite direction to estimate this trajectory from an image. Given an image x, the VAE encoder E maps it to ˆz0. When the original prompt is unavailable, DDIM inversion conditions the denoising network on an empty prompt and proceeds from ˆz0 through increasingly noisy states to estimate {ˆzt}Tt=0 .

Ring watermarks in diffusion latents. Ring watermarks are in-generation watermarks that embed a pattern into the Fourier coefficients of a diffusion latent and recover it from the generated image through DDIM inversion (Wen et al. 2023; Huang, Wu, and Wang 2024; Ci et al. 2024). For one channel of a latent zt, the shifted two-dimensional Fourier spectrum places the zero frequency at its center. Let ω index a Fourier coefficient, and let |ω| denote its distance from the zero-frequency center. An inner radius rlo and an outer radius rhi define the radial support 

R = {ω : rlo ≤ | ω| ≤ rhi }. (1) When rlo = 0 , the support is a centered low-frequency disk, while rlo > 0 gives an annular band. A seed s determines a complex pattern Ws supported on R. The band R specifies where the ring is written, while Ws specifies the coefficient values written there. During generation, the pattern is em-bedded on R at one or more latent states, and the remaining sampling steps map the modified latent to a watermarked image. Given a query image, DDIM inversion estimates the corresponding latent states, and the detector compares the coefficients observed on R with the expected pattern Ws.

# 4 Method 

IRIS is an in-generation watermarking scheme built on 

visual-semantic binding , where retention of the mark tracks displacement of the visual semantics of the image that car-ries it. From a released image and the secret key alone, the provider can verify that it generated this content. Verification requires the ring derived from the image’s semantics to match the band observed through DDIM inversion in the Fourier domain. A forged image therefore fails verification, whether it carries a transplanted mark or replaces part of the content. 

4.1 Ring Derivation from Semantics 

IRIS derives a one-time ring from the visual semantics of the image, following the derivation chain in Figure 1. The image is canonicalized and read into a CLIP embedding, the embedding is quantized under the key into a content code, and a keyed hash of the code seeds the ring. To ensure the ring stays derivable from a released image under common processing, the CLIP embedding (Radford et al. 2021) is read from a canonicalized image. The canon-icalization normalizes the image’s luminance, then blurs the image heavily and recompresses it at a low JPEG quality. The embedding is averaged over a few copies of the image that are randomly re-degraded and then canonicalized, smoothing residual jitter. The same canonicalization runs at embedding and verification, mapping an image and its JPEG-compressed or brightened copy to close CLIP embeddings. A keyed SimHash (Charikar 2002) ϕk quantizes this em-bedding into a short binary content code 

c = ϕk(x) = 1[ Pk(E(x) − μ) > 0 ] (2) where E(x) is the canonicalized embedding of the image

Because the spectrum of the real-valued latent is conjugate symmetric, a Hermitian ring survives the write whole, while a pattern without this symmetry loses its anti-symmetric half (Ci et al. 2024). The ring’s magni-tudes carry no information, and the phase survives common distortions better than the magnitude (Ruanaidh, Dowling, and Boland 1996). A Gaussian blur kernel is symmetric, and its transfer function is therefore zero-phase, attenuating the ring’s magnitudes while leaving its phases in place. JPEG compression and contrast adjustment act on the spectrum as multiplicative scaling of the magnitudes. The ring therefore remains stable through the DDIM inversion at detection. 

4.2 Ring Injection and Detection 

Injecting a semantic-bound ring faces the challenge of causal entanglement. The ring derives from the final image, yet it enters the latent before sampling finishes, and the injection may itself shift the semantics of that image. IRIS therefore keeps the watermarked image on nearly the same code as its non-watermarked counterpart, by injecting faintly and late. 

Band placement. The band R is a narrow annulus placed past the image’s content yet within reach of robust detection. Figure 2(a) motivates the selection with two measurements on 500 images. Content energy per coefficient concentrates in the low bands, where a write would alter the image. Phase survival under additive white Gaussian noise falls as the radius grows, and the outer bands lose the ring first. The ring therefore sits between the two, in the annulus holding the least content energy. It also stays narrow. The ring is rescaled to the band’s energy, and a wider band takes proportionally more injected energy, at a direct cost in PSNR. 

Injection timing and strength. The ring is blended into the late DDIM steps at an energy matched to the band’s content. Figure 2(b) tracks ˆz0, the one-step prediction of the final latent, and reports its phase coherence ρr with that latent on the write band. The band does not reach ρr ≥ 0.95 

until DDIM step 39 , and a ring written earlier would be overwritten by the steps that follow. After that point only low-energy detail is still forming there, and a late blend fuses the ring into it. IRIS therefore reuses the trajectory’s endpoint, closing the causal loop. The clean pass is the generation itself, its latent stashed at the injection start t0, just after the write band settles, and only the final T −t0 steps are replayed with the ring blended in, a small sampling overhead, timed in Table 2. # 5 Experiments 

Setup. We generate with Stable Diffusion 2.1-base (Rom-bach et al. 2022) at T =50 DDIM steps and guidance 7.5 on three prompt datasets, Stable-Diffusion-Prompts (Gustavosta 2022), DiffusionDB (Wang et al. 2023), and MS-COCO (Lin et al. 2014), covering curated prompts, user prompts, and image captions, 500 prompts each. Each prompt produces a watermarked image and its non-watermarked counterpart from the same seed. The threshold τ is the 1% -FPR quantile of detection scores on non-watermarked images. 

IRIS configuration. The ring occupies band (16 , 20] of latent channel 0, injected from t0=40 with peak strength 

α0=0 .3. The content code is a 24 -bit keyed SimHash on CLIP ViT-B/32 with the frozen cone-center. Canonicaliza-tion applies luminance normalization, Gaussian blur with 

σ=2 , JPEG at quality 25 , and K=8 test-time augmenta-tions. Detection uses radius ts=3 with L=7 low-margin bits and inverts only the 10 steps down to t0.

Baselines. We compare against three in-generation wa-termarks, Tree-Ring (Wen et al. 2023), Gaussian Shading (GS) (Yang et al. 2024b), and SEAL (Arabi et al. 2025). SEAL is the closest baseline and uses its content-aware de-tector. We include two post-hoc watermarks, Stable Signa-ture (Fernandez et al. 2023) and DwtDctSvd (Cox et al. 2007; Navas et al. 2008). Each baseline runs on the same backbone and prompts, with its threshold at the same 1% FPR.

5.2 Measuring Visual-Semantic Binding 

IRIS resists forgery with a one-time mark bound to the vi-sual semantics of the delivered image. Verification requires the ring derived from those semantics to match the band ob-served through DDIM inversion. We quantify that binding by measuring whether the derived ring or the observed band does the letting go as replacement grows. Figure 4(a) shows TPR@ 1% FPR as a patch from an MS-COCO image replaces 5% to 50% of the watermarked image. IRIS rejects more than half the images at a 10% replaced area, while the other in-generation baselines let nearly all pass. We evaluate ring survival by granting the detector the oracle code, testing whether the embedded ring still clears the matched-filter threshold against the observed band. Code survival is evaluated by recomputing the code from the at-tacked image. The code survival curve overlaps the IRIS TPR curve, while ring survival stays nearly unchanged. The de-rived ring therefore does the letting go. The observed band stays stable because every coefficient aggregates the entire frame, and a replaced patch shifts the correlation by only about the replaced fraction. The recomputed code drifts be-cause content replacement moves the CLIP embedding and with it the code. Once the code changes, the keyed hash maps it to a different derived ring, and IRIS rejects the image. 5.3 Watermark Robustness 

We evaluate the robustness of IRIS under four valuemetric and two geometric distortions, JPEG, blur, noise, bright-ness, crop, and rotation. For crop, the kept region stays at its original position and the removed border is filled by repli-cating the region’s edge pixels. For rotation, we search over a grid of candidate angles and apply the same search to non-watermarked images so that both sides of the detection threshold go through the same procedure. # 6 Conclusion 

We presented IRIS, a training-free in-generation watermark that binds a one-time ring to the visual semantics of its carrier image. The ring derives from a keyed hash of the canonical-ized CLIP embedding at the trajectory’s endpoint, enters the late DDIM steps by replay, and is recomputed at verifica-tion from the query image and the key alone. Across three prompt datasets IRIS detects reliably, preserves the gener-ated content that the other in-generation marks redraw, and alone among the compared marks withstands both forgery and regeneration. Retention holds under common distortions and decays monotonically as the visual semantics move. Past those levels the limit sits in the code reading, where heavy valuemetric distortion moves the CLIP embedding along the same axis that flags semantic change. A stronger canonical-ization or an embedding trained for valuemetric invariance would widen the retained range without touching the binding. The construction needs a deterministic invertible sampler and a frozen perceptual encoder. Deterministic flow samplers in-vert like DDIM along their ODE trajectories, and injection into them is a further direction. The acceptance cone is the se-curity parameter this framing identifies, and a proxy binding stays above one half under reprompting. References

# A Implementation Details 

Experimental environment. Generation, inversion, and the matched filter run in half precision, and the gradient forgery takes its steps through a full-precision VAE. Mea-surements run on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 under CUDA 

12 .8, PyTorch 2.7.0, and Python 3.9.

Detection scores and threshold. Each method scores an image on its own scale. We derive each threshold at a 1% 

false positive rate over a pool of 1500 non-watermarked im-ages in our runs. The IRIS phase correlation lies in [0 , 1] with threshold 0.209 . The Tree-Ring score is a negative L1 dis-tance of the inverted noise to the fixed ring, in (−∞ , 0] with threshold −50 .5. The Gaussian Shading bit accuracy lies in 

[0 , 1] at a chance level of 0.5 with threshold 0.57 . The SEAL score counts the patches whose recovered noise matches the caption-derived hash, in {0, . . . , 1024 }, and accepts an im-age when more than one patch matches. 

# B Examples of Watermarked Images 

B.1 Fidelity 

Figure 8 places IRIS beside its same-seed non-watermarked images and beside three baselines across six prompts. The non-watermarked counterpart and the IRIS image share an initial latent, leaving them close at the pixel level. The third column shows the pixel-wise difference amplified 10 ×, and the residual stays near black. Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading, and SEAL start from the same latent yet return a different image, because each writes its mark into the initial noise and lets the trajectory redraw the frame. This shows that IRIS is an in-generation watermark that keeps the main content of the image unchanged. 

B.2 Distortion, Forgery, and Removal 

Figure 9 tracks the IRIS image through one distortion, three forgeries, and one removal across six further prompts, with the detection score ρ⋆ under each image, green above the operating threshold of 0.209 and red below it. The water-marked image scores near 0.85 , additive noise leaves it above the threshold on most prompts, and regeneration leaves the mark intact, since the ring occupies a band that the redrawn detail leaves mostly untouched. The three forgeries fall be-low the threshold on each prompt. Content replacement over a tenth of the frame and reprompt both move the semantics, and the code recomputed at verification derives a different ring, and the correlation drops to the non-watermarked floor. The gradient forgery matches a cover latent to a watermarked latent yet recovers neither the code nor the ring. IRIS answers whether the delivered image is the generated image, rather than whether a fixed pattern is present. Figures 10, 11, and 12 repeat the six operations for Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading, and SEAL, each scored by its own detector against its own threshold. The three forgeries sepa-rate the methods. Tree-Ring writes a fixed ring into the initial noise, and the ring rides through a spliced patch and through regeneration under a new prompt, and its score therefore stays above the threshold on those images, where the transplanted mark verifies. Gaussian Shading recovers its message from the forgeries as well as the distortions because it is so robust that it verifies content it was not issued for. SEAL binds to the caption, and a donor patch that leaves the caption unchanged keeps its score above the threshold, while reprompt lowers the score without clearing it on most prompts. IRIS drops to the non-watermarked floor under the forgeries, because it reads the code from the delivered image at verification, and the code is sensitive to these attacks.
